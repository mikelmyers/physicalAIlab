# Coordinate Frames and Robot Motion

## Re-entry Promise

This lesson assumes you understand vectors, bearings, dot products, and cross products at an introductory level. It does not assume you have studied robotics kinematics.

A coordinate frame is just a named way to measure position and direction. The hard part is not the definition. The hard part is remembering which frame a vector belongs to before adding, comparing, or commanding it.

By the end, you should be able to explain world frames, body frames, sensor frames, and simple 2D frame transforms well enough to avoid the most common robot-motion bugs.

## Objective

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- Define a coordinate frame as an origin plus axes.
- Distinguish world, body, camera, map, and sensor frames.
- Explain why vectors must be expressed in the same frame before addition.
- Rotate 2D vectors between frames using sine and cosine.
- Translate points between frames.
- Distinguish points from free vectors during transforms.
- Build a 2D pose from position and yaw.
- Transform a point from robot/body frame into world frame.
- Transform a world point into robot/body frame.
- Interpret frame mistakes in robotics, drones, and computer vision.

## Plain-Language Intuition

Put a small arrow sticker on a robot. The robot's forward direction is its local $x$-axis. Its left direction might be local $y$. Those axes move when the robot turns.

Now draw a map on the floor. The map has its own fixed axes: east and north, or room $x$ and room $y$. Those axes do not turn with the robot.

The same physical target can have two different coordinate descriptions:

- In the world frame: "the target is at $(10,4)$ meters."
- In the robot frame: "the target is 3 meters forward and 1 meter left."

Both can be true. They answer different questions.

## Why This Idea Exists

Robots are collections of parts that each sense and act in their own local coordinates:

- A GPS receiver reports position in a global/map frame.
- A camera sees pixels in an image frame.
- An IMU measures acceleration and angular velocity in its body frame.
- A wheel encoder measures motion along the wheel or axle frame.
- A robot arm joint measures angles in link frames.
- A path planner works in a map frame.

Nothing works unless these measurements are transformed into compatible frames. Many robotics failures are not "bad math." They are frame mistakes: adding a body-frame velocity to a world-frame position, using a camera vector as if it were a map vector, or rotating in the wrong direction.

## Core Theory

### What Is A Frame?

A coordinate frame has:

1. An origin.
2. A set of axes.
3. A unit scale.
4. An orientation convention.

In 2D, a frame can be described by its origin $(x,y)$ in another frame and a yaw angle $\theta$.

### Points Versus Vectors

A **point** is a location. It depends on the origin.

A **free vector** is a direction and magnitude, like velocity or displacement. It does not depend on the origin, but it does depend on axis orientation.

When changing frames:

- Points usually need rotation and translation.
- Free vectors need rotation but not translation.

This distinction prevents many bugs.

### 2D Rotation Matrix

To rotate a vector by angle $\theta$ counter-clockwise:

$$
R(\theta)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta & \cos\theta
\end{bmatrix}
$$

So:

$$
R(\theta)
\begin{bmatrix}
x\\
y
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
x\cos\theta-y\sin\theta\\
x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta
\end{bmatrix}
$$

### Body To World Transform

Suppose a robot pose in the world is:

$$
\vec{p}_{WB}=(p_x,p_y),\quad \theta
$$

where $\theta$ is the robot body's yaw angle measured from world $x$.

A point expressed in body frame, $\vec{q}_B$, becomes a world point:

$$
\vec{q}_W=\vec{p}_{WB}+R(\theta)\vec{q}_B
$$

Read that aloud: rotate the body-frame point into world orientation, then translate by the robot's world position.

### World To Body Transform

To express a world point in the robot frame:

$$
\vec{q}_B=R(-\theta)(\vec{q}_W-\vec{p}_{WB})
$$

Read that aloud: subtract the robot position to get a displacement from robot to target, then rotate by negative yaw to express it in robot axes.

### Vector Transform

For a free vector, no translation:

$$
\vec{v}_W=R(\theta)\vec{v}_B
$$

and:

$$
\vec{v}_B=R(-\theta)\vec{v}_W
$$

Velocity, acceleration, force, and direction vectors are usually transformed this way.

### Same Frame Before Addition

You can add:

$$
\vec{v}_W+\vec{w}_W
$$

or:

$$
\vec{v}_B+\vec{w}_B
$$

You cannot safely add:

$$
\vec{v}_W+\vec{w}_B
$$

until one vector is transformed into the other's frame.

## Visual Or Mental Model

Imagine the robot carrying a tiny graph paper sheet. That sheet rotates and moves with the robot. The room also has graph paper taped to the floor.

When the robot turns 90 degrees, its "forward" no longer matches the room's positive $x$. A command of "move forward 1 m" is simple in robot frame but becomes a different vector in world frame depending on yaw.

Frame transforms are the translation between the robot's graph paper and the room's graph paper.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Rotate Body Forward Into World

A robot faces 90 degrees counter-clockwise from world $x$. Its body-frame forward velocity is:

$$
\vec{v}_B=(2,0)
$$

Rotate by 90 degrees:

$$
\vec{v}_W=R(90^\circ)(2,0)=(0,2)
$$

Forward in the body frame means north/up in the world frame.

### Example 2: Body Point To World Point

Robot world position is $(10,5)$ and yaw is 0 degrees. A sensor point is $(2,1)$ in robot frame.

Because yaw is zero:

$$
\vec{q}_W=(10,5)+(2,1)=(12,6)
$$

### Example 3: Body Point With 90 Degree Yaw

Robot world position is $(10,5)$ and yaw is 90 degrees. A body-frame point is $(2,0)$.

Rotate:

$$
R(90^\circ)(2,0)=(0,2)
$$

Translate:

$$
\vec{q}_W=(10,5)+(0,2)=(10,7)
$$

The point 2 m in front of the robot is north of it in the world.

### Example 4: World Point To Body Point

Robot is at $(10,5)$ with yaw 90 degrees. Target is at $(10,7)$ world.

Subtract robot position:

$$
(10,7)-(10,5)=(0,2)
$$

Rotate by -90 degrees:

$$
R(-90^\circ)(0,2)=(2,0)
$$

The target is 2 m forward in robot frame.

### Example 5: Free Vector Versus Point

Robot at $(100,50)$ faces 0 degrees. Body velocity is $(1,0)$ m/s.

World velocity is:

$$
R(0)(1,0)=(1,0)\text{ m/s}
$$

Do not add $(100,50)$ to velocity. Position translates; velocity does not.

### Example 6: Camera Offset

A camera is mounted 0.2 m forward and 0.1 m left of the robot origin. In robot frame:

$$
\vec{p}_{BC}=(0.2,0.1)
$$

If the robot is at $(4,3)$ with yaw 0 degrees, camera world position is:

$$
(4,3)+(0.2,0.1)=(4.2,3.1)
$$

If yaw is 90 degrees, rotate offset:

$$
R(90^\circ)(0.2,0.1)=(-0.1,0.2)
$$

Camera world position:

$$
(4,3)+(-0.1,0.2)=(3.9,3.2)
$$

### Example 7: Same Frame Before Addition

Robot body velocity is $(2,0)$ m/s. Wind in world frame is $(0,1)$ m/s. Robot yaw is 90 degrees.

First transform body velocity:

$$
\vec{v}_W=R(90^\circ)(2,0)=(0,2)
$$

Now add wind:

$$
\vec{v}_{ground,W}=(0,2)+(0,1)=(0,3)
$$

### Example 8: Frame Bug

A robot faces 90 degrees and receives a body command $(1,0)$: move forward. If software treats it as world $(1,0)$, the robot moves east instead of north. That is a frame bug, not a motor bug.

## Engineering Connections

### Mobile Robotics

The robot's local frame is natural for commands: forward, left, yaw. The map frame is natural for goals and obstacles. Navigation continuously converts between them.

### Drones

A drone has body axes, inertial/world axes, camera axes, and sometimes wind axes. Control laws must know whether velocity is body-frame or world-frame.

### Computer Vision

Pixels live in image coordinates. Rays live in camera coordinates. Objects live in world or robot coordinates. Vision systems transform between them to estimate where things are.

### Robot Arms

Each link has its own coordinate frame. Forward kinematics chains frame transforms from base to tool.

### Simulation And Game Engines

Coordinate conventions differ: some use $z$ up, some use $y$ up; some define forward as positive $x$, others negative $z$. Reading conventions is part of engineering, not paperwork.

## Common Failure Modes

1. Adding vectors expressed in different frames.
2. Translating a free vector as if it were a point.
3. Forgetting to translate a point.
4. Rotating by $\theta$ when the inverse transform requires $-\theta$.
5. Mixing degrees and radians.
6. Assuming robot forward is always world positive $x$.
7. Forgetting sensor mounting offset.
8. Confusing camera frame axes with robot frame axes.
9. Using left-handed and right-handed conventions together without conversion.
10. Failing to label data with its frame.
