# Trigonometry for Drones and Robotics

## Re-entry Promise

This lesson assumes you understand right-triangle trigonometry: sine, cosine, tangent, inverse tangent, hypotenuse, opposite, adjacent, and degree/radian mode. It does not assume you already know drone math, robot navigation, camera geometry, or vector components.

The goal here is to move from textbook triangles to field geometry. A drone does not ask, "What is the opposite side?" A robot does not ask, "Which formula should I memorize?" Real systems ask questions like:

- Where is the target relative to me?
- How wide is the camera footprint on the ground?
- How much of this motion is forward and how much is sideways?
- What heading should the robot drive?
- How far away is an object if the sensor is tilted down?
- How does an angle become a control command?

By the end, you should be able to turn angles and distances into components, heights, ground footprints, bearings, and sanity checks for simple field measurements.

## Objective

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- Convert a magnitude and heading into $x$ and $y$ components.
- Reconstruct magnitude and heading from components.
- Use tangent to estimate height, horizontal distance, and camera ground intersections.
- Estimate a drone camera footprint from altitude and field of view.
- Distinguish heading, bearing, elevation angle, depression angle, and tilt angle.
- Use `atan2` conceptually to choose the correct quadrant for a heading.
- Track degree/radian conversions in code.
- Explain why simple trig estimates need calibration in real robotics systems.
- Build a small field calculator for motion components and drone camera footprint.

## Why It Matters

Robots and drones live in coordinate systems. Their motors, cameras, sensors, and controllers constantly convert between distance, angle, and component form.

1. **Drone camera footprints.** Altitude plus field-of-view angle estimates how much ground the camera sees.

2. **Object localization.** A range sensor and bearing angle can locate a target relative to the robot.

3. **Robot motion.** A command like "move 2 meters at 30 degrees" must become horizontal and vertical components in a coordinate frame.

4. **Navigation.** Headings, waypoints, cross-track error, and approach angles are trigonometry in motion.

5. **Manipulators.** Robot arms use angles and segment lengths to locate grippers, tools, and payloads.

6. **Controls.** Many controllers do not act on raw angles. They act on projected error: forward error, sideways error, altitude error, and heading error.

This is where trigonometry begins to feel less like school math and more like instrumentation.

## Why This Idea Exists

Classical trigonometry grew from measuring inaccessible distances: stars, shorelines, towers, mountains, and ships. Robotics and drones inherit the same problem. A machine often cannot directly measure the quantity it needs. It observes an angle, a range, a pixel offset, an altitude, or a heading, then reconstructs geometry.

The modern stack may include GPS, IMUs, optical flow, lidar, stereo depth, SLAM, and sensor fusion. But the basic conversions still sit underneath:

$$
x=r\cos(\theta)
$$

$$
y=r\sin(\theta)
$$

$$
\theta=\tan^{-1}(y/x)
$$

Advanced systems add noise models and calibration. They do not remove the triangle.

## Coordinate Frames

A **coordinate frame** is a chosen way to describe position and direction.

For a simple 2D ground robot, we might say:

- $x$ points east or forward.
- $y$ points north or left.
- $\theta$ is measured counterclockwise from the positive $x$ axis.

For a drone, we might use:

- forward,
- right,
- down,
- altitude above ground,
- yaw, pitch, and roll.

The formulas depend on the frame. The math is not hard, but the naming matters. If your coordinate frame says positive $y$ is down, a positive vertical component means something different than if positive $y$ is up.

In this lesson, unless stated otherwise:

- $x$ means horizontal forward/east component,
- $y$ means horizontal left/north component,
- angles are measured from the positive $x$ direction,
- counterclockwise angles are positive,
- distances are in meters.

## Components From Magnitude And Heading

If a robot moves distance $r$ at heading $\theta$, the component form is:

$$
x=r\cos(\theta)
$$

$$
y=r\sin(\theta)
$$

This comes directly from right-triangle trig. The movement vector is the hypotenuse. The $x$ part is adjacent to the heading angle, so it uses cosine. The $y$ part is opposite the heading angle, so it uses sine.

### Example: Robot Motion

A robot moves 2.5 m at a heading of $40^\circ$.

$$
x=2.5\cos(40^\circ)\approx1.92
$$

$$
y=2.5\sin(40^\circ)\approx1.61
$$

So the robot moved about 1.92 m in the $x$ direction and 1.61 m in the $y$ direction.

This is more useful for mapping than "2.5 m at 40 degrees" because a map stores coordinates.

## Magnitude And Heading From Components

If you know components $x$ and $y$, the total magnitude is:

$$
r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}
$$

The heading is:

$$
\theta=\tan^{-1}(y/x)
$$

But there is a catch: ordinary inverse tangent alone cannot always determine the correct quadrant. For example, $y/x$ is positive when both $x$ and $y$ are positive, but also when both are negative. Those are opposite directions.

Programming languages usually provide `atan2(y, x)`, which uses both components and returns the angle in the correct quadrant.

### Example: Reconstructing A Command

A robot's desired displacement is $x=3$ m and $y=4$ m.

Magnitude:

$$
r=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5
$$

Heading:

$$
\theta=\tan^{-1}(4/3)\approx53.13^\circ
$$

So the command can be described as 5 m at about $53.13^\circ$.

If $x=-3$ and $y=-4$, the magnitude is still 5, but the heading is not $53.13^\circ$. It points into the opposite quadrant. That is why `atan2` matters.

## Bearings And Headings

A **heading** usually describes the direction a robot or drone is facing or moving. A **bearing** usually describes the direction from one point to another.

In navigation, bearings are often measured clockwise from north. In math, angles are often measured counterclockwise from the positive $x$ axis. That mismatch is a classic source of bugs.

Before using sine and cosine, ask:

1. What direction is zero degrees?
2. Does the angle increase clockwise or counterclockwise?
3. Which axis is forward, east, north, right, or down?
4. Are we using degrees or radians?

The formula is easy. The convention is the trap.

## Drone Camera Footprint

A downward-looking drone camera can be approximated with a cone or pyramid of view. In one dimension, use a right triangle.

If altitude is $h$ and the camera field-of-view half-angle is $\alpha$, the half-width of the ground footprint is:

$$
w=h\tan(\alpha)
$$

The full width is:

$$
2w=2h\tan(\alpha)
$$

### Example: Footprint Width

A drone flies at 50 m altitude. Its horizontal field of view is $70^\circ$, so the half-angle is $35^\circ$.

Half-width:

$$
w=50\tan(35^\circ)\approx35.0
$$

Full width:

$$
2w\approx70.0
$$

So the image covers about 70 m across, before lens distortion, camera tilt, terrain slope, and altitude error.

## Tilted Sensor Ground Intersection

Suppose a range sensor or camera ray is mounted at height $h$ and tilted downward by angle $\theta$ below horizontal. If it hits flat ground, the horizontal distance $d$ is:

$$
d=\frac{h}{\tan(\theta)}
$$

This is because:

$$
\tan(\theta)=\frac{h}{d}
$$

so:

$$
d=\frac{h}{\tan(\theta)}
$$

### Example: Ground Hit Distance

A camera is mounted 1.2 m above the floor and tilted down $15^\circ$.

$$
d=\frac{1.2}{\tan(15^\circ)}\approx4.48
$$

The center ray hits the floor about 4.48 m ahead.

If the floor is not flat, the mount angle is wrong, or the camera has a wide lens, this simple number becomes only a first estimate.

## Field Of View In Two Directions

Many cameras have horizontal FOV and vertical FOV.

At altitude $h$:

$$
\text{footprint width}=2h\tan(FOV_x/2)
$$

$$
\text{footprint height}=2h\tan(FOV_y/2)
$$

If a drone at 80 m altitude has horizontal FOV $60^\circ$ and vertical FOV $45^\circ$:

$$
\text{width}=2(80)\tan(30^\circ)\approx92.4
$$

$$
\text{height}=2(80)\tan(22.5^\circ)\approx66.3
$$

So the approximate ground footprint is 92.4 m by 66.3 m.

## Degrees, Radians, And Code

Most programming math libraries use radians.

In JavaScript:

```js
Math.sin(30)
```

does not mean sine of 30 degrees. It means sine of 30 radians.

Convert degrees to radians:

```js
const radians = degrees * Math.PI / 180;
```

Convert radians to degrees:

```js
const degrees = radians * 180 / Math.PI;
```

If your robot turns the wrong amount, your camera footprint is absurd, or your component values look random, angle units should be one of the first things you check.

## Calibration Reality

The formulas in this lesson are clean. Real systems are not.

Drone and robotics trig estimates may be affected by:

- camera lens distortion,
- camera mount angle error,
- sensor noise,
- rolling shutter,
- non-level ground,
- drone pitch and roll,
- wheel slip,
- coordinate-frame mismatch,
- GPS drift,
- IMU bias,
- time delay between sensor reading and motion command.

This does not make the math useless. It tells you what the math is: the ideal geometry. The real system needs calibration, filtering, and validation around that geometry.

## Common Failure Modes

1. **Degrees in radian code.** `Math.sin(30)` is not $\sin(30^\circ)$.
2. **Swapping sine and cosine.** For angle from the $x$ axis, $x$ uses cosine and $y$ uses sine.
3. **Using ordinary arctangent for all headings.** Use `atan2(y, x)` conceptually when quadrant matters.
4. **Forgetting half-angle for field of view.** A $70^\circ$ FOV has a $35^\circ$ half-angle.
5. **Confusing altitude with slant range.** Altitude is vertical. Slant range is diagonal.
6. **Ignoring coordinate conventions.** North-clockwise bearings are not the same as math angles.
7. **Treating camera footprint as exact.** Lens distortion and tilt change the shape.
8. **Forgetting units.** Component outputs keep the same distance unit as the magnitude.
9. **Ignoring sign.** Negative components carry direction.
10. **Skipping sanity checks.** A 50 m-high drone with a modest FOV should not see a 5 km-wide footprint.
