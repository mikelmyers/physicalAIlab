# Vector Arithmetic and Scaling

## Re-entry Promise

This lesson assumes you know what a vector is, how to read components, and how to compute magnitude. It does not assume vector arithmetic is automatic yet.

The previous vector lesson built the idea: a vector is a quantity with size and direction. This lesson makes vectors operational. We will add them, subtract them, scale them, compare graphical and component methods, and connect the operations to displacement, velocity, force, and robot commands.

By the end, you should be able to treat vectors as physical instructions: move this far, push this way, cancel that error, reverse this direction, and scale that command without changing its direction.

## Objective

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- Add vectors component-by-component.
- Add vectors geometrically using tip-to-tail reasoning.
- Subtract vectors as adding the opposite.
- Interpret subtraction as "from one point to another."
- Multiply a vector by a scalar.
- Explain how positive, negative, and fractional scalars affect magnitude and direction.
- Compute net displacement from multiple moves.
- Compute resultant force from component forces.
- Distinguish position vectors from displacement vectors.
- Use vector arithmetic in robot navigation and force balance.

## Why It Matters

Vector arithmetic is the accounting system for directed quantities.

1. **Navigation.** A robot's final displacement is the sum of its movement vectors.

2. **Forces.** Multiple forces combine into one resultant force.

3. **Motion planning.** A correction command can be a scaled error vector.

4. **Computer vision.** Pixel motion vectors add, subtract, and average during tracking and flow estimation.

5. **Controls.** Error vectors are scaled by gains to produce control actions.

6. **Physics.** Velocity, acceleration, electric fields, magnetic fields, and momentum obey vector arithmetic.

If you add only magnitudes, you throw away direction. Vector arithmetic keeps the direction in the calculation.

## Component Addition

If:

$$
\vec{a}=(a_x,a_y)
$$

and:

$$
\vec{b}=(b_x,b_y)
$$

then:

$$
\vec{a}+\vec{b}=(a_x+b_x,\ a_y+b_y)
$$

In 3D:

$$
(a_x,a_y,a_z)+(b_x,b_y,b_z)=(a_x+b_x,\ a_y+b_y,\ a_z+b_z)
$$

The rule is simple because each component represents motion along one independent axis.

The word independent matters. Moving 3 meters east does not secretly move you north. Moving 4 meters north does not secretly move you east. So the east parts can be added to east parts, and the north parts can be added to north parts.

That is why:

$$
(3,4)+(-1,2)=(2,6)
$$

means:

- east/west total: $3+(-1)=2$,
- north/south total: $4+2=6$.

Vector addition is bookkeeping for independent directions.

### Example: Robot Moves

A robot moves:

$$
\vec{a}=(3,4)
$$

then:

$$
\vec{b}=(-1,2)
$$

Net displacement:

$$
\vec{a}+\vec{b}=(3+(-1),4+2)=(2,6)
$$

The final displacement from the starting point is 2 units in $x$ and 6 units in $y$.

## Geometric Addition

To add vectors geometrically, place the tail of the second vector at the head of the first. The result is the arrow from the original tail to the final head.

This is called **tip-to-tail** addition.

The component rule and the geometric rule agree. Components are the arithmetic version of the same picture.

You can see the agreement by walking the components. If $\vec{a}=(a_x,a_y)$ and $\vec{b}=(b_x,b_y)$, tip-to-tail motion first moves $a_x$ in $x$ and $a_y$ in $y$, then moves $b_x$ in $x$ and $b_y$ in $y$. The total $x$ motion is $a_x+b_x$, and the total $y$ motion is $a_y+b_y$.

So the geometry and the arithmetic are not separate rules. They are the same displacement story told two ways.

### Why Magnitudes Do Not Usually Add

If you walk 3 m east and then 4 m north, your path length is 7 m, but your displacement magnitude is 5 m.

The displacement vector is:

$$
(3,4)
$$

Its magnitude is:

$$
\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5
$$

Vector addition tracks where you end up, not how much path you walked.

## Vector Negatives

The negative of a vector points in the opposite direction with the same magnitude.

If:

$$
\vec{v}=(3,-5)
$$

then:

$$
-\vec{v}=(-3,5)
$$

Geometrically, the arrow flips around.

Adding a vector and its negative gives the zero vector:

$$
\vec{v}+(-\vec{v})=\vec{0}
$$

## Subtraction

Vector subtraction means adding the opposite:

$$
\vec{a}-\vec{b}=\vec{a}+(-\vec{b})
$$

Component-wise:

$$
\vec{a}-\vec{b}=(a_x-b_x,\ a_y-b_y)
$$

Subtraction answers a direction question: "What vector takes me from the second object to the first object?"

That is why order matters. The vector $\vec{a}-\vec{b}$ points from $\vec{b}$ to $\vec{a}$. The vector $\vec{b}-\vec{a}$ points the other way.

### Displacement Between Points

If point $P=(2,3)$ and point $Q=(8,7)$, the vector from $P$ to $Q$ is:

$$
\vec{PQ}=Q-P=(8-2,\ 7-3)=(6,4)
$$

This is one of the most important uses of subtraction:

> destination minus start gives displacement.

Reverse the order and you reverse the direction:

$$
\vec{QP}=P-Q=(-6,-4)
$$

## Scalar Multiplication

A scalar is an ordinary number. Multiplying a vector by a scalar scales its components:

$$
k\vec{v}=k(v_x,v_y)=(kv_x,kv_y)
$$

If:

$$
\vec{v}=(2,-3)
$$

then:

$$
4\vec{v}=(8,-12)
$$

### What Scalars Do

- If $k>1$, the vector gets longer.
- If $0<k<1$, the vector gets shorter.
- If $k=0$, the result is the zero vector.
- If $k<0$, the vector reverses direction and scales by $|k|$.

For example:

$$
-2(3,4)=(-6,-8)
$$

The magnitude doubles and direction reverses.

## Unit Vectors And Scaling

A unit vector has magnitude 1.

To create a unit vector in the direction of $\vec{v}$:

$$
\hat{v}=\frac{\vec{v}}{|\vec{v}|}
$$

If $\vec{v}=(3,4)$, then $|\vec{v}|=5$, so:

$$
\hat{v}=(3/5,4/5)
$$

To make a vector of magnitude 10 in that direction:

$$
10\hat{v}=(6,8)
$$

This pattern appears constantly in robotics: compute a direction, normalize it, then scale it by desired speed.

There is one important exception: you cannot normalize the zero vector.

If:

$$
\vec{v}=(0,0)
$$

then:

$$
|\vec{v}|=0
$$

and dividing by zero is undefined. Physically, the zero vector has no direction. A robot that is already exactly at its target cannot infer a direction to move from the error vector alone.

## Weighted Sums And Control

A weighted sum is a sum of scaled vectors:

$$
k_1\vec{a}+k_2\vec{b}
$$

This is how many control and planning systems combine goals:

- move toward target,
- avoid obstacle,
- stay near path,
- slow down near goal.

A simple command might be:

$$
\vec{u}=0.8\vec{e}-0.3\vec{o}
$$

where $\vec{e}$ points toward the target and $\vec{o}$ points toward an obstacle. The subtraction means "move away from the obstacle direction."

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Net Displacement

A drone moves:

$$
(4,1),\quad (-2,3),\quad (0,-5)
$$

Sum:

$$
(4-2+0,\ 1+3-5)=(2,-1)
$$

Net displacement is $(2,-1)$.

Magnitude:

$$
\sqrt{2^2+(-1)^2}=\sqrt5
$$

### Example 2: Force Resultant

Three forces act on a small robot bracket:

$$
\vec{F}_1=(10,0)\text{ N}
$$

$$
\vec{F}_2=(-4,3)\text{ N}
$$

$$
\vec{F}_3=(0,-2)\text{ N}
$$

Resultant:

$$
\vec{F}=(10-4+0,\ 0+3-2)=(6,1)\text{ N}
$$

The net force points mostly in the positive $x$ direction.

### Example 3: Error Vector

A robot is at:

$$
P=(2,5)
$$

Target is:

$$
T=(9,1)
$$

Error vector from robot to target:

$$
\vec{e}=T-P=(9-2,\ 1-5)=(7,-4)
$$

A proportional controller might command:

$$
\vec{u}=0.2\vec{e}=(1.4,-0.8)
$$

The command points toward the target but is scaled down.

## Common Failure Modes

1. **Adding magnitudes instead of components.**
2. **Subtracting in the wrong order.** Destination minus start gives displacement.
3. **Confusing position with displacement.** A point is a location; a displacement is a change.
4. **Forgetting negative scalar reverses direction.**
5. **Normalizing the zero vector.** The zero vector has no direction.
6. **Dropping units.** Force components stay in newtons; displacement components stay in meters.
7. **Thinking tip-to-tail changes the vector.** Sliding a vector without rotating or stretching it does not change it.
8. **Assuming path length equals displacement magnitude.**
9. **Scaling only one component accidentally.**
10. **Using component addition across mismatched coordinate frames.** All vectors must be expressed in the same frame.
