# Circles: Arcs, Sectors, and Tangents

## Re-entry Promise

This lesson assumes you understand points, lines, angles, triangle similarity, the Pythagorean theorem, and basic perimeter and area. It assumes you can convert between degrees and radians, or at least follow the conversion formulas. It does not assume you remember circle theorems.

We will treat circles as the geometry of rotation. A wheel rolling forward, a pulley turning, a robot rotating around a pivot, a drone flying a circular path, a camera sweeping a field of view, and a gear tooth moving along a pitch circle are all circle problems. The circle is not just "round shape." It is the path of a point at fixed distance from a center.

By the end, you should be able to compute circumference, area, arc length, sector area, chord relationships, tangent relationships, and basic circle angles. You should also understand why radians are the clean language for circular motion.

## Objective

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- Define center, radius, diameter, chord, secant, tangent, arc, sector, central angle, and inscribed angle.
- Compute circumference and area from radius or diameter.
- Compute arc length using $s=r\theta$ when $\theta$ is in radians.
- Compute sector area using $A=\frac{1}{2}r^2\theta$ when $\theta$ is in radians.
- Convert degree-based arc and sector problems into fractional-turn formulas.
- Use tangent-radius perpendicularity.
- Use the inscribed angle theorem: an inscribed angle measures half its intercepted arc.
- Distinguish arc length from chord length.
- Apply circle geometry to wheels, gears, pulleys, camera fields, robot turning, and circular workspaces.
- Avoid common failures: mixing degrees and radians, using diameter as radius, confusing arc and chord, and assuming tangent lines cut through the circle.

## Why It Matters

Circle geometry is the geometry of rotation, curvature, and repeated motion.

1. **Wheels and odometry.** A wheel with radius $r$ travels one circumference per revolution. Encoder counts become distance through circumference and arc length.

2. **Pulleys and belts.** Belt length around pulleys depends on arcs and straight tangent segments. A small error in radius changes travel per revolution.

3. **Gears.** Gear pitch circles, angular spacing, tooth motion, and angular velocity all depend on circle geometry.

4. **Robotics.** A differential-drive robot turns along circular arcs. A robot arm joint sweeps an arc. A workspace boundary may be circular.

5. **Drones and navigation.** Holding patterns, orbit shots, turn radii, sensor sweep cones, and circular exclusion zones use arcs and sectors.

6. **Computer vision.** Camera fields of view often form angular sectors. Circular lenses, radial distortion, and Hough circle detection all use circle concepts.

7. **Manufacturing.** Holes, fillets, rounded corners, bolt circles, arcs, and tangencies are everywhere in CAD drawings.

Circle geometry is where "angle" becomes "distance traveled along a curve."

## Why This Idea Exists

People studied circles because the sky, wheels, pottery, calendars, gears, arches, and navigation demanded it. Ancient mathematicians measured circular arcs, approximated $\pi$, and related angles to chord lengths long before modern calculus.

Euclid's *Elements*, Book III, develops circle theorems about chords, tangents, and angles. Archimedes gave deep bounds on $\pi$ and studied areas and arcs. Later, trigonometry grew from chord tables in circles. Calculus eventually made circular motion one of the central examples of rates of change.

The modern reason circles matter is even broader: periodic motion, rotation matrices, sine and cosine, complex numbers, Fourier analysis, control systems, AC circuits, and wave physics all inherit circle structure.

This lesson is the elementary doorway into that world.

## Plain-Language Intuition

A circle is all points the same distance from one center. That fixed distance is the **radius**.

If you walk around a circular track, your distance from the center stays constant. Your position changes because your angle changes. The farther you are from the center, the more distance you travel for the same turn.

That is the heart of arc length:

$$
s = r\theta
$$

where:

- $s$ is arc length,
- $r$ is radius,
- $\theta$ is angle in radians.

Double the radius and the same angle sweeps twice the distance. Double the angle and you sweep twice the distance. This is why radians are so useful: they make the relationship exact and simple.

## Core Theory

### Basic Parts Of A Circle

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Center | Fixed point all circle points are measured from |
| Radius | Segment from center to circle |
| Diameter | Segment through center connecting two circle points |
| Chord | Segment connecting two circle points |
| Secant | Line crossing the circle in two points |
| Tangent | Line touching the circle at exactly one point |
| Arc | Connected piece of circumference |
| Sector | Wedge-shaped region bounded by two radii and an arc |
| Central angle | Angle with vertex at circle center |
| Inscribed angle | Angle with vertex on circle, sides are chords |

Diameter is twice radius:

$$
d=2r
$$

### Circumference And Area

Circumference is boundary length:

$$
C=2\pi r
$$

Area is surface coverage:

$$
A=\pi r^2
$$

If diameter is given:

$$
C=\pi d
$$

and:

$$
A=\pi\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^2
$$

### Arc Length In Radians

If a central angle $\theta$ is measured in radians, arc length is:

$$
s=r\theta
$$

This formula is one of the main reasons radians exist. A radian is defined so that angle equals arc length divided by radius:

$$
\theta = \frac{s}{r}
$$

Rearrange:

$$
s=r\theta
$$

If $\theta$ is in degrees, convert first:

$$
\theta_{\text{rad}}=\theta_{\text{deg}}\frac{\pi}{180}
$$

### Arc Length As Fraction Of Circumference

If you want to stay in degrees, use fraction of a full turn:

$$
s=\frac{\theta}{360^\circ}(2\pi r)
$$

For example, a $90^\circ$ arc is one quarter of a circle.

### Sector Area

If $\theta$ is in radians:

$$
A_{\text{sector}}=\frac{1}{2}r^2\theta
$$

If $\theta$ is in degrees:

$$
A_{\text{sector}}=\frac{\theta}{360^\circ}\pi r^2
$$

Sector area is the same fraction of circle area that the central angle is of a full turn.

### Chords And Arcs

A **chord** is straight. An **arc** is curved. They connect the same endpoints but have different lengths unless the endpoints are the same.

For a central angle less than $180^\circ$, arc length is longer than chord length because the arc bends outward.

The longest chord is the diameter.

### Tangents

A tangent line touches a circle at one point. The radius to the tangent point is perpendicular to the tangent:

$$
\text{radius} \perp \text{tangent}
$$

This matters in wheels, belts, cams, and contact geometry. At the instant a wheel contacts the ground, the radius to the contact point is perpendicular to the ground tangent.

### Inscribed Angles

An inscribed angle has its vertex on the circle and intercepts an arc. Its measure is half the measure of the intercepted arc.

If an inscribed angle intercepts a $100^\circ$ arc, the angle is:

$$
50^\circ
$$

A special case: an angle inscribed in a semicircle is a right angle, because the intercepted arc is $180^\circ$ and half of that is $90^\circ$.

## Visual Or Mental Models

### Model 1: Wheel Tape

Imagine wrapping tape around a wheel. The tape length around the whole wheel is circumference. A partial piece of tape along the rim is an arc.

### Model 2: Pizza Slice

A sector is a pizza slice: two radii and a curved crust. The crust length is arc length. The slice surface is sector area.

### Model 3: Rotation Budget

A full turn is $2\pi$ radians. Half turn is $\pi$. Quarter turn is $\pi/2$. Once you think this way, rotation math becomes cleaner.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Circumference

A wheel has radius 0.25 m. Find circumference.

$$
C=2\pi r=2\pi(0.25)=0.5\pi
$$

So:

$$
C\approx1.57\text{ m}
$$

One full wheel revolution moves the wheel about 1.57 m, ignoring slip.

### Example 2: Area

A circular landing pad has diameter 10 m. Find area.

Radius is:

$$
r=5\text{ m}
$$

Area:

$$
A=\pi r^2=\pi(5^2)=25\pi
$$

So:

$$
A\approx78.54\text{ m}^2
$$

### Example 3: Arc Length In Radians

A robot arm link endpoint is 0.6 m from the joint. The joint rotates by 1.2 radians. How far does the endpoint travel along its arc?

$$
s=r\theta=(0.6)(1.2)=0.72
$$

Arc length:

$$
0.72\text{ m}
$$

### Example 4: Arc Length In Degrees

A pulley of radius 4 cm turns through $90^\circ$. Find arc length.

Use fraction of circumference:

$$
s=\frac{90}{360}(2\pi\cdot4)
$$

$$
s=\frac{1}{4}(8\pi)=2\pi
$$

Arc length:

$$
2\pi\text{ cm}\approx6.28\text{ cm}
$$

### Example 5: Sector Area

A sprinkler covers a $60^\circ$ sector with radius 12 m. Find watered area.

$$
A=\frac{60}{360}\pi(12^2)
$$

$$
A=\frac{1}{6}\pi(144)=24\pi
$$

So:

$$
A\approx75.40\text{ m}^2
$$

### Example 6: Sector Area In Radians

A lidar sector has radius 5 m and angle 0.8 radians. Find sector area.

$$
A=\frac{1}{2}r^2\theta
$$

$$
A=\frac{1}{2}(25)(0.8)=10
$$

Area:

$$
10\text{ m}^2
$$

### Example 7: Tangent Radius

A line is tangent to a circle at point $T$. The center is $O$. What is the angle between $\overline{OT}$ and the tangent line?

The radius to the tangent point is perpendicular to the tangent:

$$
90^\circ
$$

### Example 8: Inscribed Angle

An inscribed angle intercepts an arc of $132^\circ$. Find the angle.

$$
\text{angle}=\frac{1}{2}(132^\circ)=66^\circ
$$

### Example 9: Chord vs Arc

Two points on a circle are separated by a $60^\circ$ central angle. Radius is 10 cm.

Arc length:

$$
s=\frac{60}{360}(2\pi\cdot10)=\frac{10\pi}{3}\approx10.47\text{ cm}
$$

The chord is shorter. In fact, for a $60^\circ$ central angle, the chord and two radii make an equilateral triangle, so the chord is 10 cm.

### Example 10: Failure Mode - Diameter As Radius

A circular hole has diameter 8 mm. A student computes area as:

$$
\pi(8)^2=64\pi
$$

Wrong. The radius is 4 mm:

$$
A=\pi(4)^2=16\pi\text{ mm}^2
$$

## Engineering Connections

### Wheel Odometry

If wheel radius is $r$, one revolution travels $2\pi r$. If an encoder reports half a revolution, distance is $\pi r$. More generally:

$$
s=r\theta
$$

where $\theta$ is wheel rotation in radians.

### Differential Drive Turns

When a robot turns, each wheel may travel along a circular arc. The outside wheel travels farther than the inside wheel because its radius from the turn center is larger.

### Camera Field Sectors

A camera or lidar has an angular field of view. At range $r$, that field covers an arc or sector. The farther the range, the wider the covered arc for the same angle.

### Gears And Pulleys

Gear teeth are spaced around pitch circles. Belt contact wraps around arcs. Tangent lines between pulleys are straight belt spans. Circle geometry is the diagram behind the mechanism.

### CAD Fillets

A fillet is an arc tangent to two edges. The radius controls how rounded the corner is. Tangency ensures a smooth transition.

## Common Failure Modes

1. **Using degrees in $s=r\theta$.** That formula requires radians.

2. **Using diameter as radius.** Radius is half of diameter.

3. **Confusing arc length and chord length.** Arc is curved boundary; chord is straight segment.

4. **Confusing sector area and arc length.** Sector area is square units; arc length is length units.

5. **Forgetting tangent perpendicularity.** Radius to tangent point is $90^\circ$ to the tangent.

6. **Assuming every line touching a drawing is tangent.** Tangency must be stated, marked, constructed, or proved.

7. **Rounding $\pi$ too early.** Keep exact forms like $24\pi$ until the final approximation.
