# Rational Functions and Asymptotes

## Re-entry Promise

This lesson assumes you can factor polynomials and understand domain restrictions. It does not assume asymptotes already make intuitive sense.

A rational function is a ratio of polynomials. Ratios are everywhere in engineering: gain, efficiency, impedance, transfer functions, rates, normalized errors, and signal-to-noise. But ratios can break when the denominator is zero, and their long-range behavior can approach lines or levels without touching them.

By the end, you should be able to find domains, holes, vertical asymptotes, horizontal asymptotes, and slant asymptotes for common rational functions.

## Objective

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

## Plain-Language Intuition

A rational function is a fraction where the top and bottom are polynomials:

$$
R(x)=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}
$$

The denominator is dangerous. If it becomes zero, the expression is undefined. Sometimes that undefined point is a removable hole. Sometimes the function shoots upward or downward without bound, creating a vertical asymptote.

An asymptote is a line the graph approaches. It describes behavior near forbidden inputs or far away from the origin.

## Why This Idea Exists

Ratios model relationships where one quantity is normalized by another:

- Output/input gain.
- Voltage/current impedance.
- Signal/noise ratio.
- Transfer functions in control systems.
- Efficiency as useful output divided by input.
- Error divided by scale.
- Camera projection formulas with depth in the denominator.

Rational functions teach a critical engineering habit: denominators are not passive. They control singularities, instability, and limits.

## Core Theory

### Rational Function Definition

A rational function has form:

$$
R(x)=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}
$$

where $P$ and $Q$ are polynomials and $Q(x)\ne0$.

The condition $Q(x)\ne0$ is part of the definition, not a footnote. A rational function is not "the simplified expression you get after canceling." It is the original input-output rule together with the inputs where that rule is allowed to run.

That distinction is why holes exist. Algebra can simplify a formula, but it cannot erase the fact that the original machine had an illegal input.

### Domain Restrictions

The domain excludes values that make the denominator zero.

Example:

$$
R(x)=\frac{x+1}{x-3}
$$

Domain:

$$
x\ne3
$$

### Holes

A hole occurs when a factor cancels from numerator and denominator.

Example:

$$
R(x)=\frac{(x-2)(x+1)}{x-2}
$$

For $x\ne2$, this simplifies to:

$$
x+1
$$

But $x=2$ is still excluded from the original function. The graph has a hole at:

$$
(2,3)
$$

The phrase "still excluded" is the important part. The original function asks you to divide by $x-2$. At $x=2$, that denominator is zero. Even though the simplified expression $x+1$ can be evaluated at 2, the original rational function cannot.

So a hole means:

1. the original formula is undefined at that input,
2. nearby values behave like a simpler function,
3. the graph is missing exactly one point.

You can think of a hole as domain memory. The simplified expression remembers the shape, but the original expression remembers the forbidden input.

### Vertical Asymptotes

A vertical asymptote occurs when a denominator factor remains after simplification.

Example:

$$
R(x)=\frac{x+1}{x-3}
$$

Vertical asymptote:

$$
x=3
$$

Why does an uncanceled denominator factor create an asymptote instead of a hole?

Near $x=3$, the denominator $x-3$ becomes very small. The numerator $x+1$ becomes close to 4, which is ordinary and nonzero. A nonzero number divided by a tiny number becomes huge in magnitude:

$$
\frac{\text{ordinary nonzero}}{\text{tiny}}\approx\text{huge}
$$

That is the mechanism behind a vertical asymptote. The function does not merely become undefined at one point; it grows without bound near that point.

Contrast this with a hole:

$$
\frac{(x-2)(x+1)}{x-2}
$$

Near $x=2$, the numerator and denominator both contain the same tiny factor. For nearby values other than 2, those factors cancel and the output stays near 3. That is why the graph has a missing point rather than an infinite wall.

### One-Sided Behavior Near A Vertical Asymptote

Finding $x=3$ as a vertical asymptote is not the whole story. You should also ask what happens from the left and from the right.

For:

$$
R(x)=\frac{1}{x-3}
$$

as $x\to3^+$, the denominator is a tiny positive number, so:

$$
R(x)\to+\infty
$$

as $x\to3^-$, the denominator is a tiny negative number, so:

$$
R(x)\to-\infty
$$

This sign reasoning is more important than memorizing graph shapes. It tells you which way the graph shoots near a forbidden input.

### Horizontal Asymptotes

Compare degrees of numerator and denominator.

Let numerator degree be $n$, denominator degree be $d$.

- If $n<d$, horizontal asymptote is $y=0$.
- If $n=d$, horizontal asymptote is ratio of leading coefficients.
- If $n>d$, there is no horizontal asymptote. There may be a slant or polynomial asymptote.

Examples:

$$
\frac{3x+1}{x^2+1}\to0
$$

$$
\frac{2x^2+1}{5x^2-3}\to\frac25
$$

The degree rules come from dominant terms. Far away from zero, the highest-power term overwhelms the lower-power terms.

Example:

$$
\frac{3x+1}{x^2+1}
$$

For very large $|x|$, the $3x$ term dominates the numerator and $x^2$ dominates the denominator:

$$
\frac{3x+1}{x^2+1}\approx\frac{3x}{x^2}=\frac{3}{x}
$$

As $|x|$ grows, $\frac{3}{x}$ approaches 0. That is why the horizontal asymptote is $y=0$.

For equal degrees:

$$
\frac{2x^2+1}{5x^2-3}
$$

the dominant terms are:

$$
\frac{2x^2}{5x^2}
$$

The $x^2$ cancels in the far-away comparison:

$$
\frac{2x^2}{5x^2}=\frac25
$$

That is why equal-degree rational functions approach the ratio of leading coefficients.

If the numerator degree is larger, the numerator grows too fast for the denominator to hold the function near a horizontal level. Then division reveals the polynomial or slant behavior that remains.

### Slant Asymptote

If numerator degree is exactly one more than denominator degree, divide.

Example:

$$
\frac{x^2+3x+5}{x+1}=x+2+\frac{3}{x+1}
$$

As $|x|$ grows, the fraction term approaches 0, so the slant asymptote is:

$$
y=x+2
$$

This is not a special graphing trick. It is the division algorithm again:

$$
\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}=\text{quotient}+\frac{\text{remainder}}{Q(x)}
$$

If the remainder has lower degree than $Q(x)$, then:

$$
\frac{\text{remainder}}{Q(x)}\to0
$$

as $|x|$ grows. The rational function gets closer and closer to the quotient. When the quotient is a line, that line is the slant asymptote.

In the example:

$$
\frac{x^2+3x+5}{x+1}=x+2+\frac{3}{x+1}
$$

The term $\frac{3}{x+1}$ becomes tiny far away, so the graph behaves like:

$$
y=x+2
$$

The asymptote is the long-range trend left after the shrinking error term disappears.

### Intercepts

$x$-intercepts occur when numerator is zero and denominator is not zero.

$y$-intercept occurs at $x=0$ if defined.

### Graphing Workflow

For a rational function:

$$
R(x)=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}
$$

use this sequence:

1. Factor numerator and denominator.
2. Record every denominator zero as excluded from the domain.
3. Cancel common factors only after recording exclusions.
4. Canceled factors become holes.
5. Uncanceled denominator factors become vertical asymptotes.
6. Compare degrees or divide to find horizontal/slant/polynomial asymptotes.
7. Find intercepts from the simplified expression, while respecting exclusions.
8. Test signs on intervals split by holes, intercepts, and vertical asymptotes.
9. Sketch behavior near asymptotes and far away.

This workflow is deliberately slow at first. Speed comes after the order becomes automatic.

## Visual Or Mental Model

The denominator creates forbidden places. The numerator creates zeros. Common factors create holes. Uncanceled denominator factors create vertical asymptotes. Degree comparison controls far-away behavior.

Before graphing, factor first.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Domain

Find domain:

$$
R(x)=\frac{2x+1}{x^2-9}
$$

Denominator:

$$
x^2-9=(x-3)(x+3)
$$

Domain:

$$
x\ne3,\quad x\ne-3
$$

### Example 2: Hole

$$
R(x)=\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}
$$

Factor:

$$
\frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1}
$$

Hole at $x=1$. Simplified function is $x+1$, so hole point is:

$$
(1,2)
$$

### Example 3: Vertical Asymptote

$$
R(x)=\frac{x+2}{x-4}
$$

No common factor. Vertical asymptote:

$$
x=4
$$

Check one-sided behavior:

- As $x\to4^+$, the denominator is positive and the numerator is near 6, so $R(x)\to+\infty$.
- As $x\to4^-$, the denominator is negative and the numerator is near 6, so $R(x)\to-\infty$.

### Example 4: Horizontal Asymptote Degree Smaller On Top

$$
R(x)=\frac{5x+1}{x^2+3}
$$

Numerator degree 1, denominator degree 2. Horizontal asymptote:

$$
y=0
$$

### Example 5: Horizontal Asymptote Equal Degree

$$
R(x)=\frac{6x^2-1}{3x^2+4x}
$$

Same degree. Ratio of leading coefficients:

$$
y=\frac63=2
$$

### Example 6: Slant Asymptote

$$
R(x)=\frac{x^2+3x+5}{x+1}
$$

Divide:

$$
R(x)=x+2+\frac{3}{x+1}
$$

Slant asymptote:

$$
y=x+2
$$

### Example 7: Hole Plus Vertical Asymptote

$$
R(x)=\frac{(x-2)(x+1)}{(x-2)(x-5)}
$$

Cancel $x-2$ for simplified behavior:

$$
\frac{x+1}{x-5}
$$

Hole at $x=2$. Its $y$ value:

$$
\frac{2+1}{2-5}=-1
$$

Vertical asymptote:

$$
x=5
$$

### Example 8: Transfer Function

A simple transfer function:

$$
H(s)=\frac{s+2}{s+5}
$$

Zero at $s=-2$, pole at $s=-5$. The denominator root is a pole, not just an algebraic inconvenience. In control systems, pole locations shape stability and response.

### Example 9: Full Graphing Workflow

Analyze:

$$
R(x)=\frac{(x-1)(x+2)}{(x-1)(x-3)}
$$

Domain exclusions:

$$
x\ne1,\quad x\ne3
$$

Cancel the common factor $x-1$:

$$
R(x)=\frac{x+2}{x-3},\quad x\ne1,3
$$

Hole:

At $x=1$:

$$
y=\frac{1+2}{1-3}=-\frac32
$$

so the hole is:

$$
\left(1,-\frac32\right)
$$

Vertical asymptote:

$$
x=3
$$

Horizontal asymptote:

Degrees are equal, leading coefficient ratio is 1, so:

$$
y=1
$$

$x$-intercept:

$$
x=-2
$$

$y$-intercept:

$$
R(0)=\frac{2}{3}
$$

This is enough structure to sketch the graph without plotting hundreds of points.

## Engineering Connections

### Control Systems

Transfer functions are rational functions. Denominator roots are poles; numerator roots are zeros.

### Signal Processing

Filters use rational functions in frequency or complex variables. Asymptotic behavior describes gain at high or low frequency.

### Circuits

Impedance and gain formulas often become rational expressions of frequency.

### Computer Vision

Perspective projection divides by depth. When depth approaches zero, behavior becomes singular.

### Normalized Error

Ratios like error/scale are rational-like. Small denominators can amplify noise or create instability.

## Common Failure Modes

1. Canceling terms instead of factors.
2. Forgetting canceled denominator zeros are still excluded.
3. Calling a hole a vertical asymptote.
4. Missing a vertical asymptote after partial simplification.
5. Applying horizontal asymptote rules before simplifying.
6. Forgetting slant asymptote when numerator degree is one higher.
7. Treating denominator zero as output zero.
8. Ignoring physical meaning of denominator approaching zero.
9. Dropping domain restrictions after algebraic simplification.
10. Confusing zeros of numerator with zeros of denominator.
11. Finding an asymptote but never checking one-sided sign behavior.
12. Sketching before recording domain exclusions.
