# Integer Exponents and Exponent Rules

## Re-entry Promise

Exponents are the third tier of arithmetic. After addition and subtraction (the additive layer) and multiplication and division (the multiplicative layer), exponentiation is the *power* layer — repeated multiplication that produces dizzyingly fast growth. Every exponential phenomenon in nature and technology — compound interest, population growth, radioactive decay, RC charging, Moore's law, neural network parameter counts — speaks the language of exponents.

This lesson assumes you have completed fraction and decimal arithmetic (lessons 6–10) and that you know how signed numbers behave (lesson 11–12). It does not assume you remember the laws of exponents; we will derive each one from scratch. By the end you will know not only *what* the laws say but *why* they take the shape they do — including the genuinely puzzling cases of zero exponents and negative exponents, both of which require careful argument to motivate.

The next two lessons take this further: roots and radicals (lesson 14, the inverse of exponentiation for non-negative bases) and scientific notation (lesson 15, exponents as the engineering tool for very large and very small numbers). Without solid exponent fluency, neither of those works. So this is foundational — do it carefully.

## Objective

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- Define $x^n$ for any nonnegative integer $n$ and any real $x$.
- State and apply the **seven laws of integer exponents**: product rule, quotient rule, power-of-a-power, power-of-a-product, power-of-a-quotient, zero exponent, and negative exponent.
- Explain *why* $x^0 = 1$ for any nonzero $x$ — as a consequence of the product rule, not an arbitrary convention.
- Explain *why* $x^{-n} = \frac{1}{x^n}$ — also a consequence of the product rule.
- Recognize and avoid the most common exponent errors: distributing across sums ($(a + b)^2 \ne a^2 + b^2$), confusing $a \cdot b^n$ with $(ab)^n$, mis-parsing $-3^2$ versus $(-3)^2$.
- Compute with **scientific notation** in a basic way (full treatment in lesson 15).
- Apply exponent reasoning to engineering: powers of two in computing, decibels and powers of ten in signal processing, RC time constants in electronics, compound growth in finance, Moore's law and exponential growth/decay generally.
- Express comfort with **exponent towers** and recognize when a "small" exponent is actually astronomically large (e.g., $2^{64}$, $10^{80}$).

## Why It Matters

Exponents quantify the most important *non-linear* phenomenon in the natural world. Two examples for the doubting:

1. **Compound interest.** A small interest rate, applied repeatedly, produces astonishing wealth (or debt) over time. \$1 at 5% for 200 years becomes \$1 · 1.05^200 ≈ \$17,292 — about 17,000× the principal.

2. **Computer addressing.** A 32-bit memory address space holds $2^{32} \approx 4.3 \times 10^9$ unique addresses. A 64-bit address space holds $2^{64} \approx 1.8 \times 10^{19}$ — *four billion times larger*. Doubling the bits multiplies the address space by 4 billion. This is why we moved from 32-bit to 64-bit computing.

A few more places exponents show up:

3. **Decibels and audio.** Decibel scale: $20 \log_{10}(V_2/V_1)$. A 6 dB amplifier gain is a factor of $2$ in voltage. A 60 dB gain is a factor of $1000$. The whole audio engineering vocabulary lives in logarithms of powers.

4. **RC charging.** A capacitor voltage rises as $V(t) = V_0(1 - e^{-t/RC})$ — an exponential approach to the supply voltage. Every microcontroller designer must understand exponential charging.

5. **Memory and storage.** "Kilobyte" was traditionally $2^{10} = 1024$ bytes (now officially "kibibyte"); a "gigabyte" is $2^{30}$. Powers of two are the literal storage unit.

6. **Neural networks.** A modern LLM has $10^{11}$ to $10^{12}$ parameters. Training requires processing $10^{12}$ to $10^{13}$ tokens. These numbers do not fit on a calculator screen without exponents.

7. **Drone battery discharge.** Lithium cells discharge approximately exponentially under load (with corrections). Battery models routinely use exponent expressions.

8. **Cryptographic security.** A 256-bit symmetric key has $2^{256} \approx 10^{77}$ possible values. A brute-force search of this space is infeasible by *any* future technology — about the number of atoms in the observable universe. The exponent matters because it determines what *cannot* be done.

9. **Sensor noise.** Quantization noise of an $N$-bit ADC is approximately $\frac{V_\text{range}}{2^N \sqrt{12}}$. The exponent $N$ determines the noise floor.

Exponents are the way humans communicate scale across many orders of magnitude. Get them right, and you can reason about anything from atomic energy levels to galactic distances using the same arithmetic.

## Why This Idea Exists

The idea of repeated multiplication is ancient. Euclid (around 300 BCE) used "squared" and "cubed" for $x^2$ and $x^3$ — terms that explicitly invoke geometric pictures. A square with side $s$ has area $s^2$; a cube with side $s$ has volume $s^3$.

The notation $a^n$ for general powers was introduced by **René Descartes** in *La Géométrie* (1637). Before Descartes, mathematicians wrote out powers in full (sometimes with elaborate Latin abbreviations like *quadratum*, *cubus*, *quadrato-quadratum*, etc.). Descartes' compact notation revolutionized algebra. The convention $a^n = a \cdot a \cdot \ldots \cdot a$ ($n$ factors) is *his* gift to us.

**Negative exponents.** John Wallis and Isaac Newton (mid-1600s) extended exponents to negative integers, observing that the exponent rules force $a^{-n} = 1/a^n$ to make $a^0 = 1$ consistent. This was a deduction, not a definition — a recurring pattern in mathematics where extending a notation correctly is *forced* by demanding internal consistency.

**Fractional exponents.** Wallis also extended to fractional exponents ($a^{1/2} = \sqrt{a}$), giving the full edifice of real exponents we will build up over the next few lessons.

**Logarithms** were invented around 1614 by **John Napier**, specifically to convert multiplication into addition (since $\log(ab) = \log a + \log b$). Napier's tables let astronomers and navigators multiply 6-digit numbers by adding their logarithms — a massive labor saving in the pre-calculator age. Logarithms are *inverse exponents*, and we will study them in their own right later.

**Decibels and engineering.** The decibel scale (introduced by Bell Labs around 1924) uses logarithms of power ratios because the human ear hears sound *exponentially*. A 10× increase in sound pressure feels like a fixed increment, not a 10× increment. This perceptual logarithmic compression is why decibels are useful: a few decibels' difference is what we actually perceive.

**Information theory.** Claude Shannon (1948) defined information in bits, where the number of bits required to specify one of $n$ messages is $\log_2 n$. This is *defining* information as the inverse of an exponent. Modern compression, error correction, and cryptography are built on this idea.

**Modern: exponential growth of compute.** The modern phenomenon known as Moore's law (Gordon Moore, 1965) is *literally* an exponential growth law: transistor counts double every 18–24 months. This has played out for 60 years, taking us from kilobyte-scale memories (1970) to terabyte-scale memories (2020) and beyond.

Exponents are not just an arithmetic technique. They are the conceptual machinery for thinking about anything that grows or shrinks by *factors* rather than by *amounts*. That distinction matters wherever processes feed back on themselves: physics, biology, finance, computing, learning.

## Plain-Language Intuition

### Exponents as Repeated Multiplication

$x^n$ means "$x$ multiplied by itself $n$ times" (for positive integer $n$).

- $5^1 = 5$
- $5^2 = 5 \cdot 5 = 25$
- $5^3 = 5 \cdot 5 \cdot 5 = 125$
- $5^4 = 5 \cdot 5 \cdot 5 \cdot 5 = 625$

The pattern: each time you increase the exponent by 1, you multiply by another copy of the base.

### Reading Exponents Aloud

- $x^2$: "$x$ squared" or "$x$ to the second power."
- $x^3$: "$x$ cubed" or "$x$ to the third power."
- $x^4$: "$x$ to the fourth power" or "$x$ to the fourth."
- $x^n$: "$x$ to the $n$-th power."

"Squared" and "cubed" are special; they come from geometry (a square of side $s$ has area $s^2$, a cube of side $s$ has volume $s^3$).

### The Multiplicative Structure

If exponents are repeated multiplication, then multiplying powers should *add* the exponents — because joining "$x$ three times" with "$x$ five times" gives "$x$ eight times":

$$x^3 \cdot x^5 = (x \cdot x \cdot x)(x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot x) = x^8$$

That is the most important rule. Most of the others follow by similar reasoning.

### Two Counter-Intuitive Cases

**Zero exponent.** $x^0 = 1$ for any nonzero $x$. Why isn't $x^0 = 0$ (since "multiply $x$ zero times" sounds like nothing)? Because we want the product rule $x^m \cdot x^n = x^{m+n}$ to keep working when $n = 0$: $x^m \cdot x^0 = x^{m+0} = x^m$. This forces $x^0 = 1$.

**Negative exponent.** $x^{-n} = \frac{1}{x^n}$. Why? Again to keep the product rule consistent: $x^n \cdot x^{-n} = x^0 = 1$, which forces $x^{-n} = 1/x^n$.

So zero and negative exponents are *not* arbitrary conventions — they are the unique values that keep the laws of exponents consistent. This is a hallmark of good mathematics: notation chosen to make the rules simple.

## Core Theory

### Definition

For a real number $x$ and a positive integer $n$:

$$x^n = \underbrace{x \cdot x \cdot \ldots \cdot x}_{n \text{ factors}}$$

The number $x$ is the **base**; the number $n$ is the **exponent** or **power**.

### Special Values

- $x^1 = x$ for any $x$.
- $x^0 = 1$ for any $x \ne 0$. ($0^0$ is a special case; we will discuss.)
- $0^n = 0$ for any positive integer $n$.
- $1^n = 1$ for any $n$.
- $(-1)^n = 1$ if $n$ is even, $-1$ if $n$ is odd.

### The Seven Laws of Integer Exponents

For any nonzero base(s) and integer exponents (positive, zero, or negative):

**Law 1: Product Rule.**

$$x^m \cdot x^n = x^{m + n}$$

Combining factors with the same base — the exponents add.

Example: $3^4 \cdot 3^2 = 3^{4+2} = 3^6 = 729$.

**Law 2: Quotient Rule.**

$$\frac{x^m}{x^n} = x^{m - n}$$

Dividing factors with the same base — the exponents subtract.

Example: $\frac{5^7}{5^3} = 5^{7-3} = 5^4 = 625$.

**Law 3: Power of a Power.**

$$(x^m)^n = x^{m \cdot n}$$

Raising a power to a power — the exponents multiply.

Example: $(2^3)^4 = 2^{3 \cdot 4} = 2^{12} = 4096$.

**Law 4: Power of a Product.**

$$(xy)^n = x^n \cdot y^n$$

Raising a product to a power — distribute the exponent.

Example: $(2 \cdot 5)^3 = 2^3 \cdot 5^3 = 8 \cdot 125 = 1000 = 10^3$. ✓

**Law 5: Power of a Quotient.**

$$\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^n = \frac{x^n}{y^n}$$

Same idea, with division.

Example: $\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^2 = \frac{9}{16}$.

**Law 6: Zero Exponent.**

$$x^0 = 1 \text{ for } x \ne 0$$

**Law 7: Negative Exponent.**

$$x^{-n} = \frac{1}{x^n}$$

Equivalently: $\frac{1}{x^{-n}} = x^n$.

Example: $2^{-3} = \frac{1}{2^3} = \frac{1}{8}$.

### Why $x^0 = 1$

Use the quotient rule with $m = n$:

$$\frac{x^n}{x^n} = x^{n - n} = x^0$$

But $\frac{x^n}{x^n} = 1$ for any nonzero $x$ (anything divided by itself is 1). So $x^0 = 1$. ∎

This is not a convention; it is *forced* by demanding that the quotient rule keep working.

### Why $x^{-n} = \frac{1}{x^n}$

Use the product rule with one of the exponents negative, asking what value $x^{-n}$ must have to satisfy $x^n \cdot x^{-n} = x^0 = 1$:

$$x^n \cdot x^{-n} = x^{n + (-n)} = x^0 = 1$$

So $x^{-n}$ must be the multiplicative inverse of $x^n$, i.e., $\frac{1}{x^n}$. ∎

Again, *forced* by the laws — not an arbitrary definition.

### The Awkward Case: $0^0$

Some textbooks say $0^0 = 1$ (the "combinatorial convention"); others leave it undefined.

The arguments for $0^0 = 1$: it makes the binomial theorem work (the $k=0$ term in $(1+x)^n$ is $\binom{n}{0} x^0 = 1$), and the empty product is conventionally 1.

The argument against: $0^n = 0$ for any $n > 0$, while $x^0 = 1$ for $x \ne 0$. These two "limits" disagree at $(0, 0)$; the function $x^y$ has a discontinuity there.

For our purposes, take $0^0 = 1$ as a convention used in combinatorics and computer science, with the caveat that "0 to the 0" is a slightly weird case that you should never rely on in calculus or analysis without explicit justification.

### Powers of Negative Numbers

For any positive integer $n$:

- $(-x)^n = (-1)^n \cdot x^n$
- If $n$ is even: $(-x)^n = x^n$ (positive).
- If $n$ is odd: $(-x)^n = -x^n$ (negative).

Examples:

- $(-3)^2 = 9$ (even power).
- $(-3)^3 = -27$ (odd power).
- $(-3)^4 = 81$ (even).
- $(-3)^5 = -243$ (odd).

Remember the parenthesization rule: $-3^2 = -9$ (exponent first, then negate), but $(-3)^2 = 9$ (parenthesized base includes the negative).

### Order of Operations Reminder

PEMDAS: **P**arentheses, **E**xponents, **M**ultiplication and **D**ivision (left to right), **A**ddition and **S**ubtraction (left to right).

Exponents bind tighter than multiplication, which binds tighter than addition. So $3 + 2 \cdot 5^2 = 3 + 2 \cdot 25 = 3 + 50 = 53$, *not* $3 + (10)^2 = 103$.

### What Does NOT Distribute

These are the most common errors. None of these is a valid exponent rule:

- $(a + b)^n \ne a^n + b^n$ in general.
- $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$ (the binomial expansion).
- $\sqrt{a + b} \ne \sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}$.

The product rules ($(xy)^n = x^n y^n$) work only over multiplication and division, not over addition or subtraction. This is the most-violated rule in algebra.

A quick numerical check usually disqualifies a wrong distribution. $(2 + 3)^2 = 25$, but $2^2 + 3^2 = 4 + 9 = 13$. They are not equal.

### Powers of Two and Powers of Ten

You should know by heart:

$2^0 = 1$, $2^1 = 2$, $2^2 = 4$, $2^3 = 8$, $2^4 = 16$, $2^5 = 32$, $2^6 = 64$, $2^7 = 128$, $2^8 = 256$, $2^9 = 512$, $2^{10} = 1024$.

$2^{10} \approx 10^3$ (off by 2.4%), which is the basis of the rough engineering shortcut "kilo means $2^{10}$ in computing."

$2^{20} \approx 10^6$ (mega), $2^{30} \approx 10^9$ (giga), $2^{40} \approx 10^{12}$ (tera). Each $2^{10}$ is about $10^3$.

$10^0 = 1$, $10^1 = 10$, $10^2 = 100$, $10^3 = 1000$ (kilo), $10^6$ (mega), $10^9$ (giga), $10^{12}$ (tera), $10^{15}$ (peta), $10^{18}$ (exa).

$10^{-3}$ (milli), $10^{-6}$ (micro), $10^{-9}$ (nano), $10^{-12}$ (pico), $10^{-15}$ (femto), $10^{-18}$ (atto).

Memorize these prefixes. They appear in every engineering data sheet.

## Visual or Mental Model

### Model 1: Exponential Doubling

Picture a chessboard. Place one grain of rice on the first square. Two grains on the second. Four on the third. Continue doubling. By the 64th square, you would have $2^{63} \approx 9.2 \times 10^{18}$ grains — more rice than has ever existed.

This is the classic "wheat and chessboard" story from medieval Persia. It is the most vivid demonstration of exponential growth's unintuitive speed. Doubling at each step *seems* like a small change, but 64 doublings is a number larger than the entire global rice harvest of all human history.

### Model 2: Powers-of-Ten Slide

Imagine a slide where each click of a dial changes the value by a factor of 10:

```
... 10^-3, 10^-2, 10^-1, 10^0, 10^1, 10^2, 10^3, ...
    0.001, 0.01,  0.1,   1,    10,   100,  1000, ...
```

This is the *order of magnitude* scale. Engineering and science live on this scale. Going from $10^3$ to $10^6$ is "three orders of magnitude" — a thousand-fold change.

The famous *Powers of Ten* film (Ray and Charles Eames, 1968, on YouTube) starts at a 1-meter-square view in Chicago, zooms out by powers of 10 every few seconds until reaching the cosmic scale ($10^{25}$ m), then zooms in to subatomic scale ($10^{-15}$ m). The whole physical universe spans about 40 powers of ten.

### Model 3: The Exponent Lattice (Diagonal Multiplication)

Picture a 2D grid where rows are the base $a$ and columns are the exponent $n$. Each cell is $a^n$:

| | $n=0$ | $n=1$ | $n=2$ | $n=3$ | $n=4$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $a=2$ | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 | 16 |
| $a=3$ | 1 | 3 | 9 | 27 | 81 |
| $a=5$ | 1 | 5 | 25 | 125 | 625 |
| $a=10$ | 1 | 10 | 100 | 1000 | 10000 |

Going right by 1 multiplies by the base. Going down by 1 in the base row would *also* multiply by something (the inter-base ratio). Powers naturally form a 2D structure.

### Model 4: The Repeated-Halving Number Line

For negative powers: each step left on the exponent axis *divides* by the base. Starting from $2^4 = 16$:

$$2^4 = 16 \to 2^3 = 8 \to 2^2 = 4 \to 2^1 = 2 \to 2^0 = 1 \to 2^{-1} = \frac{1}{2} \to 2^{-2} = \frac{1}{4} \to \ldots$$

Each step halves the previous value. This is the natural mental model for negative exponents.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Product Rule

$4^2 \cdot 4^5 = ?$

$4^{2+5} = 4^7 = 16384$.

### Example 2 — Quotient Rule

$\frac{6^9}{6^4} = ?$

$6^{9-4} = 6^5 = 7776$.

### Example 3 — Power of a Power

$(7^2)^3 = ?$

$7^{2 \cdot 3} = 7^6 = 117649$.

### Example 4 — Negative Exponent

$3^{-4} = ?$

$\frac{1}{3^4} = \frac{1}{81}$.

### Example 5 — Combined: Numerator and Denominator with Exponents

$\frac{2^3 \cdot 5^2}{2 \cdot 5} = ?$

Use the quotient rule on each base: $\frac{2^3}{2^1} \cdot \frac{5^2}{5^1} = 2^{3-1} \cdot 5^{2-1} = 2^2 \cdot 5 = 4 \cdot 5 = 20$.

### Example 6 — Power of a Product

$(2 \cdot 3)^4 = ?$

$2^4 \cdot 3^4 = 16 \cdot 81 = 1296$.

Check: $6^4 = 1296$. ✓

### Example 7 — Zero Exponent

$73^0 = 1$. (Any nonzero base to the zero power.)

$(100^0 + 5)^0 = (1 + 5)^0 = 6^0 = 1$. (Even when the base is a sum.)

### Example 8 — Negative Base with Even Exponent

$(-4)^2 = (-4) \cdot (-4) = 16$.

$(-2)^6 = 64$ (even power, positive result).

### Example 9 — Negative Base with Odd Exponent

$(-2)^5 = (-2) \cdot (-2) \cdot (-2) \cdot (-2) \cdot (-2)$. The five factors of $-2$ give $-32$.

### Example 10 — $-x^n$ vs $(-x)^n$

$-2^4$: exponent first. $2^4 = 16$, then negate: $-16$.

$(-2)^4$: parenthesized base includes the negative. $(-2)(-2)(-2)(-2) = 16$.

Different answers from a single character of difference.

### Example 11 — Compound Use of Laws

Simplify $\frac{(2x^3)^2 \cdot x^{-4}}{x^5}$.

Apply Law 4 to the numerator: $(2x^3)^2 = 4 x^6$.

Now: $\frac{4 x^6 \cdot x^{-4}}{x^5}$.

Combine using product rule in numerator: $x^6 \cdot x^{-4} = x^{6-4} = x^2$.

So: $\frac{4 x^2}{x^5}$.

Quotient rule: $x^{2-5} = x^{-3}$.

Final: $\frac{4}{x^3}$.

### Example 12 — Powers of Two

$2^{10} = ?$

$2^{10} = 1024$. Memorize this — it is one of the most-used constants in computing.

$2^{16} = ?$. By the product rule, $2^{16} = 2^{10} \cdot 2^6 = 1024 \cdot 64 = 65{,}536$.

$2^{32} = ?$. $2^{32} = 2^{16} \cdot 2^{16} = 65{,}536^2$. Compute: $65{,}536^2 = 4{,}294{,}967{,}296$. About 4.3 billion.

### Example 13 — Battery Energy Scale

A drone battery has capacity 80 Wh. In joules (1 Wh = 3600 J): $80 \cdot 3600 = 288{,}000$ J $= 2.88 \times 10^5$ J.

To express in scientific notation: $2.88 \times 10^5$ J. Reading exponents is easier than reading 288000.

### Example 14 — Light Wavelength

Visible light has wavelength about $500$ nm = $500 \times 10^{-9}$ m = $5 \times 10^{-7}$ m.

The exponent $-7$ says "the wavelength has 7 decimal places of zeros." This is much easier to keep track of than $0.0000005$.

### Example 15 — Memory Sizes

A 4 GB stick of RAM is $4 \cdot 2^{30}$ bytes (using the binary "giga" $\approx 10^9$). That is about $4.3 \times 10^9$ bytes.

A 1 TB hard drive is $10^{12}$ bytes (in decimal SI), or $2^{40} \approx 1.1 \times 10^{12}$ bytes (in binary, often called "tebibyte"). The factor-of-1.1 discrepancy between "manufacturer GB" and "OS-reported GiB" is just $\frac{10^9}{2^{30}} \approx 0.93$, a 7% gap.

### Example 16 — Decibels and Power Ratios

10 dB = factor of 10. 20 dB = factor of 100. 30 dB = factor of 1000. 100 dB = factor of $10^{10}$.

For voltage (not power), the formula is $20 \log_{10}(V_2/V_1)$, so 6 dB ≈ factor of 2 in voltage (since $\log_{10}(2) \approx 0.301$ and $20 \cdot 0.301 \approx 6$). A 60 dB voltage gain is a factor of $10^3 = 1000$.

### Example 17 — Failure Mode: Distributing Across Addition

A student computes $(3 + 4)^2 = 3^2 + 4^2 = 9 + 16 = 25$.

But $(3 + 4)^2 = 7^2 = 49$, not 25.

The correct expansion: $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$. So $(3 + 4)^2 = 9 + 24 + 16 = 49$. ✓

The "exponent distributes" instinct is wrong over addition. Always check by plugging in numbers.

### Example 18 — Failure Mode: Wrong Sign

A student computes $-5^2 = 25$.

Wrong. $-5^2 = -(5^2) = -25$. The exponent binds tighter than unary minus. For $+25$, write $(-5)^2$.

### Example 19 — Failure Mode: Misapplying Quotient Rule

A student computes $\frac{x^3}{y^2}$ as $x^{3-2} = x^1$ (using the quotient rule incorrectly).

Wrong: the quotient rule applies only when the *bases* are the same. $\frac{x^3}{y^2}$ has different bases ($x$ and $y$), so the exponents do not combine; the expression is already in simplest form.

### Example 20 — Failure Mode: Forgetting Negative Exponent Means Reciprocal

A student computes $2^{-3}$ as $-8$.

Wrong: a negative exponent means reciprocal, not "negate the result." $2^{-3} = \frac{1}{2^3} = \frac{1}{8}$, not $-8$.

## Engineering Connections

### Binary and Memory

Every byte is 8 bits. With $b$ bits, you can represent $2^b$ distinct values. Memory capacities are powers of 2:

- 1 KiB = $2^{10}$ bytes = 1024 bytes (kibibyte, IEC binary prefix).
- 1 MiB = $2^{20}$ bytes ≈ $10^6$.
- 1 GiB = $2^{30}$ bytes ≈ $10^9$.
- 1 TiB = $2^{40}$ bytes ≈ $10^{12}$.

The SI decimal versions (KB, MB, GB, TB) use $10^3, 10^6, 10^9, 10^{12}$ respectively. The two definitions overlap closely but not exactly. Most operating systems show "GiB" (binary) for RAM sizes and "GB" (decimal) for disk sizes, leading to mild confusion.

### IP Addresses

IPv4 uses 32-bit addresses, giving $2^{32} \approx 4.3$ billion unique IPs. The 1980s designers thought this was enormous. It is in fact too few for the modern internet — hence the move to IPv6 with 128-bit addresses, $2^{128} \approx 3.4 \times 10^{38}$. The size jump is *not* "four times bigger" but $2^{96}$ times bigger — roughly $10^{29}$, more than enough to address every grain of sand on Earth.

### Cryptographic Key Sizes

- AES-128: $2^{128} \approx 3.4 \times 10^{38}$ possible keys.
- AES-256: $2^{256} \approx 1.2 \times 10^{77}$ possible keys.

A brute-force attack on AES-128 is currently infeasible. AES-256 is infeasible even with hypothetical quantum computers (which Grover's algorithm would let attack at $\sqrt{N}$ speed, i.e., $2^{128}$ ops — still infeasible).

The exponent matters because it tells you what is *not* possible to compute, no matter how fast hardware gets.

### RC Time Constants

A capacitor charging through a resistor has voltage:

$$V(t) = V_\text{supply}(1 - e^{-t/RC})$$

The time constant $\tau = RC$ tells you how fast the voltage approaches its final value. After one $\tau$, the voltage is $1 - e^{-1} \approx 63.2\%$ of full. After 5 $\tau$, it's $1 - e^{-5} \approx 99.3\%$.

For $R = 10$ kΩ and $C = 10$ µF: $\tau = 10^4 \cdot 10^{-5} = 10^{-1}$ s = 100 ms. The capacitor reaches 99% of full after about 500 ms.

These calculations use exponentials throughout (lesson 22 of this module covers $e^x$ formally). For now, the integer exponents in $RC$ give you the time scale.

### Frequency Scales

Audio: ~20 Hz to 20 kHz (human hearing). FM radio: ~88-108 MHz. Wi-Fi: 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz, 6 GHz. Visible light: $\sim 10^{14}$ Hz. Gamma rays: $\sim 10^{19}$ Hz.

Across 18 orders of magnitude in frequency, the equations of electromagnetism are the same. The exponents are just the scale parameter.

### Computer Performance

CPU clock speeds: ~1 GHz to ~5 GHz, or about $10^9$ operations per second per core. A modern multicore CPU can do roughly $10^{10}$ operations per second. A high-end GPU does $10^{13}$ floating-point ops per second (FLOPS). A modern AI supercomputer cluster does $10^{18}$ FLOPS (exaFLOPS).

The growth of compute is itself exponential (Moore's law). 60 years of $2^{20}$ growth ($\approx 10^6$) is what the modern computing era has delivered.

### Drone Battery Discharge

A lithium-polymer cell discharging at constant current shows roughly:

$$V(t) \approx V_\text{nominal} - I \cdot R_\text{int} - K \cdot \text{SoC}^p$$

where SoC is state of charge and $p$ is an exponent close to 1 but not exactly. Battery models like the Shepherd model or the Nernst equation use exponents to capture the nonlinear discharge curve.

A drone's flight-time prediction relies on these exponential models.

## Build Task

Write `integer_exponents.py` (or your language equivalent).

Required functions:

1. `power(base, exponent)` — implement $a^n$ for nonnegative integer $n$, using repeated multiplication.
