# Logarithmic Functions

## Re-entry Promise

This lesson assumes you understand exponential functions. It does not assume logarithms feel friendly.

A logarithm answers an exponent question. If exponentials ask "what value do I get after repeated multiplication?", logarithms ask "how many multiplications did it take?"

By the end, you should be able to read logarithms as inverse exponentials, evaluate simple logs, use log rules, understand log scales, and solve basic exponential equations.

## Objective

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- Define a logarithm as an exponent.
- Convert between exponential and logarithmic form.
- Evaluate common logarithms mentally.
- Explain domain restrictions for logarithmic functions.
- Understand base 10, base 2, and natural logs.
- Use product, quotient, and power rules.
- Solve simple exponential equations.
- Interpret logarithmic scales such as decibels, pH, and orders of magnitude.
- Explain why logs compress large ranges.
- Avoid common log-rule mistakes.

## Plain-Language Intuition

The statement:

$$
\log_2(8)=3
$$

means:

> 2 raised to what power gives 8?

The answer is 3 because:

$$
2^3=8
$$

Logs are not new arithmetic. They are inverse exponentials.

## Why This Idea Exists

Logarithms exist because exponentials hide the variable in the exponent. If:

$$
2^t=64
$$

you need a way to solve for $t$. A logarithm does that.

Logs also compress scale. Engineering often deals with quantities spanning huge ranges: sound intensity, signal power, acidity, earthquake energy, algorithmic search spaces, model likelihoods. Log scales let humans compare multiplicative differences using additive distances.

## Core Theory

### Definition

For $b>0$, $b\ne1$:

$$
\log_b(x)=y
$$

means:

$$
b^y=x
$$

The input $x$ must be positive.

This definition is the whole subject. A logarithm is not a new kind of number; it is the exponent needed to produce a number from a chosen base.

Example:

$$
\log_2(32)=5
$$

because 5 is the exponent that makes:

$$
2^5=32
$$

The base matters. $\log_2(32)$ asks for the power of 2. $\log_{10}(32)$ asks for the power of 10. They are different questions.

### Common Bases

- $\log_{10}(x)$: common log, used for powers of 10 and many engineering scales.
- $\log_2(x)$: binary log, used in computing and information.
- $\ln(x)=\log_e(x)$: natural log, used in calculus and continuous growth.

### Domain And Range

For:

$$
f(x)=\log_b(x)
$$

domain:

$$
x>0
$$

range:

$$
\text{all real numbers}
$$

Logs of zero or negative numbers are not real in this course.

### Inverse Relationship

Logs undo exponentials:

$$
\log_b(b^x)=x
$$

and:

$$
b^{\log_b(x)}=x
$$

for $x>0$.

These identities are just inverse-function logic.

If you start with an exponent $x$, build the power $b^x$, and then ask "what exponent made this?", you get back $x$:

$$
\log_b(b^x)=x
$$

If you start with a positive value $x$, ask what exponent creates it, and then raise $b$ to that exponent, you get back the original value:

$$
b^{\log_b(x)}=x
$$

This is the same undoing relationship as squaring and square root, except logarithms undo exponentials.

### Product Rule

$$
\log_b(MN)=\log_b(M)+\log_b(N)
$$

Multiplication inside becomes addition outside.

Here is why.

Let:

$$
\log_b(M)=m
$$

and:

$$
\log_b(N)=n
$$

That means:

$$
M=b^m,\quad N=b^n
$$

Multiply:

$$
MN=b^m b^n
$$

Same-base powers multiply by adding exponents:

$$
MN=b^{m+n}
$$

So the exponent needed to produce $MN$ is $m+n$:

$$
\log_b(MN)=m+n=\log_b(M)+\log_b(N)
$$

Logs turn multiplication into addition because exponents turn multiplication of same-base powers into addition.

### Quotient Rule

$$
\log_b(M/N)=\log_b(M)-\log_b(N)
$$

Division inside becomes subtraction outside.

The quotient rule comes from the same exponent law:

$$
\frac{b^m}{b^n}=b^{m-n}
$$

If $M=b^m$ and $N=b^n$, then:

$$
\frac{M}{N}=b^{m-n}
$$

so:

$$
\log_b(M/N)=m-n
$$

### Power Rule

$$
\log_b(M^p)=p\log_b(M)
$$

Exponents inside come down as multipliers.

Again, let:

$$
M=b^m
$$

Then:

$$
M^p=(b^m)^p=b^{mp}
$$

So:

$$
\log_b(M^p)=mp=p\log_b(M)
$$

The log rules are not arbitrary. They are exponent rules translated through the inverse relationship between exponentials and logarithms.

### Solving Exponential Equations

If:

$$
3^t=20
$$

then:

$$
t=\log_3(20)
$$

Using natural logs:

$$
t=\frac{\ln 20}{\ln 3}
$$

The formula:

$$
\log_b(x)=\frac{\ln x}{\ln b}
$$

is the change-of-base formula.

Why does change of base work?

Start with:

$$
t=\log_b(x)
$$

That means:

$$
b^t=x
$$

Take natural log of both sides:

$$
\ln(b^t)=\ln x
$$

Use the power rule:

$$
t\ln b=\ln x
$$

Solve for $t$:

$$
t=\frac{\ln x}{\ln b}
$$

Since $t=\log_b(x)$:

$$
\log_b(x)=\frac{\ln x}{\ln b}
$$

This matters because calculators and programming languages usually provide $\ln$ and $\log_{10}$, not every possible base.

## Visual Or Mental Model

An exponential graph grows quickly. A logarithmic graph grows slowly.

A log scale turns equal multiplicative jumps into equal additive spacing.

On a base-10 log scale:

$$
1,\ 10,\ 100,\ 1000
$$

are evenly spaced because their logs are:

$$
0,\ 1,\ 2,\ 3
$$

That is why logs are so useful for huge ranges.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Convert Form

$$
\log_2(32)=5
$$

means:

$$
2^5=32
$$

### Example 2: Evaluate

$$
\log_{10}(1000)=3
$$

because:

$$
10^3=1000
$$

### Example 3: Natural Log

$$
\ln(e^4)=4
$$

because $\ln$ means log base $e$.

### Example 4: Product Rule

$$
\log_2(8\cdot4)=\log_2(8)+\log_2(4)=3+2=5
$$

And indeed:

$$
8\cdot4=32,\quad \log_2(32)=5
$$

### Example 5: Power Rule

$$
\log_3(9^2)=2\log_3(9)=2(2)=4
$$

### Example 6: Solve For Time

A quantity doubles every 5 hours:

$$
A(t)=100\cdot2^{t/5}
$$

When does it reach 800?

$$
800=100\cdot2^{t/5}
$$

$$
8=2^{t/5}
$$

$$
3=t/5
$$

$$
t=15
$$

### Example 7: Solve With Change Of Base

Solve:

$$
3^t=50
$$

$$
t=\frac{\ln50}{\ln3}\approx3.56
$$

### Example 8: Decibels

Decibels use logs because power ratios span huge ranges.

Power gain in dB:

$$
G_{dB}=10\log_{10}\left(\frac{P_{out}}{P_{in}}\right)
$$

If power ratio is 100:

$$
G_{dB}=10\log_{10}(100)=20\text{ dB}
$$

## Engineering Connections

### Computing

$\log_2(n)$ measures how many halvings or doublings are involved. Binary search on $n$ sorted items takes about $\log_2(n)$ comparisons.

### Signal Processing

Decibels turn multiplicative power ratios into additive values.

### Chemistry And Sensors

pH is a logarithmic scale for hydrogen ion concentration.

### Machine Learning

Log likelihood turns products of probabilities into sums, which are easier and more stable to compute.

### Robotics And Telemetry

Log plots reveal multiplicative improvement, decay rates, and order-of-magnitude error reduction.

## Common Failure Modes

1. Forgetting that logs require positive inputs.
2. Treating $\log(a+b)$ as $\log a+\log b$.
3. Forgetting the base.
4. Mixing common log and natural log without noticing.
5. Thinking logs make negative numbers in the real system.
6. Misusing the power rule.
7. Forgetting change of base when calculator lacks a desired base.
8. Treating log scale distances as ordinary linear distances.
9. Taking log of a quantity with units without forming a ratio or using a reference.
10. Forgetting logs are inverse exponentials.
