# Inverse Trigonometric Functions

## Re-entry Promise

This lesson assumes you know right-triangle trigonometry, the unit circle, and basic function notation. It does not assume you already understand why inverse sine and inverse cosine have restricted outputs, or why `atan2` is different from ordinary arctangent.

Regular trig answers:

> Given an angle, what ratio or coordinate do I get?

Inverse trig answers:

> Given a ratio or components, what angle could have produced it?

That sounds simple until you remember that sine, cosine, and tangent repeat. Many angles can have the same sine. Many angles can have the same cosine. Tangent repeats every $180^\circ$. To make inverse trig functions behave like functions, we must choose principal output ranges.

By the end, you should be able to use arcsin, arccos, arctan, and `atan2` correctly in triangles, vectors, robotics, drones, sensors, and code.

## Objective

## Why It Matters

Inverse trig is how measurements become angles.

1. **Robot heading.** Given desired displacement components, compute the heading angle.

2. **Drone line of sight.** Given altitude and horizontal distance, compute camera depression angle.

3. **Ramp design.** Given rise and run, compute slope angle.

4. **Sensor mounting.** Given height and target distance, compute tilt angle.

5. **Vector reconstruction.** Given $x$ and $y$ components, compute magnitude and direction.

6. **Controls.** Controllers often need angular error from component error.

7. **Computer graphics.** Camera orientation, object rotation, and aiming all need angle recovery.

The forward trig functions project angles into ratios. Inverse trig reconstructs angles from those projections.

## Why Inverse Trig Is Subtle

Consider sine.

$$
\sin(30^\circ)=1/2
$$

But:

$$
\sin(150^\circ)=1/2
$$

If you ask:

$$
\sin^{-1}(1/2)=?
$$

there are multiple possible angles. A function must give one output for one input. So inverse sine chooses a principal angle:

$$
\sin^{-1}(1/2)=30^\circ
$$

The other possible angles are still real solutions in equation-solving contexts, but they are not the principal output of arcsin.

This is the core idea: inverse trig functions return a chosen angle, not every possible angle.

## Notation Warning

The notation $\sin^{-1}(x)$ usually means arcsine:

$$
\sin^{-1}(x)=\arcsin(x)
$$

It does **not** mean:

$$
\frac{1}{\sin(x)}
$$

That reciprocal is cosecant:

$$
\csc(x)=\frac{1}{\sin(x)}
$$

This notation is unfortunate, but common. In programming, inverse sine is usually named something like `asin`.

## Principal Ranges

To make inverse trig functions work as functions, each one has a restricted output range.

### Arcsine

$$
y=\arcsin(x)
$$

means:

$$
\sin(y)=x
$$

with:

$$
-\frac{\pi}{2}\le y\le\frac{\pi}{2}
$$

In degrees:

$$
-90^\circ\le y\le90^\circ
$$

Input domain:

$$
-1\le x\le1
$$

### Arccosine

$$
y=\arccos(x)
$$

means:

$$
\cos(y)=x
$$

with:

$$
0\le y\le\pi
$$

In degrees:

$$
0^\circ\le y\le180^\circ
$$

Input domain:

$$
-1\le x\le1
$$

### Arctangent

$$
y=\arctan(x)
$$

means:

$$
\tan(y)=x
$$

with:

$$
-\frac{\pi}{2}<y<\frac{\pi}{2}
$$

In degrees:

$$
-90^\circ<y<90^\circ
$$

Input domain: all real numbers.

Arctangent has no problem accepting large inputs, because tangent can produce any real number. But ordinary arctangent cannot distinguish quadrant I from quadrant III using a ratio alone.

## Choosing The Right Inverse Function

Use arcsin when you know:

$$
\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}
$$

Use arccos when you know:

$$
\frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}
$$

Use arctan when you know:

$$
\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}
$$

Use `atan2(y, x)` when you know signed components and need the quadrant-correct direction.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Ramp Angle

A ramp rises 0.8 m over a horizontal run of 6 m.

Use tangent:

$$
\tan\theta=\frac{0.8}{6}
$$

So:

$$
\theta=\arctan(0.8/6)\approx7.59^\circ
$$

The ramp angle is about $7.59^\circ$.

### Example 2: Drone Elevation Angle

A drone is 40 m high and 100 m horizontally from a marker. What is the angle of elevation from the marker?

Opposite is 40. Adjacent is 100.

$$
\theta=\arctan(40/100)\approx21.80^\circ
$$

### Example 3: Angle From Sine

A cable has length 13 m and vertical rise 5 m. What angle does it make with the horizontal?

Opposite is 5. Hypotenuse is 13.

$$
\theta=\arcsin(5/13)\approx22.62^\circ
$$

Because this is a physical right-triangle angle, the principal arcsin output is the angle we want.

### Example 4: Heading From Components

A robot displacement is:

$$
x=-3,\quad y=4
$$

Magnitude:

$$
r=\sqrt{(-3)^2+4^2}=5
$$

The vector is in quadrant II. Ordinary:

$$
\arctan(y/x)=\arctan(-4/3)
$$

returns a negative principal angle, which points the wrong way for the vector.

Use:

```js
Math.atan2(y, x)
```

Conceptually:

$$
\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(4,-3)\approx126.87^\circ
$$

That is the correct quadrant II heading.

## `atan2` In Plain Language

Ordinary arctangent sees only a slope:

$$
\frac{y}{x}
$$

But slopes repeat across opposite quadrants. A vector with $(x,y)=(3,4)$ and one with $(-3,-4)$ have the same ratio $y/x=4/3$, but they point in opposite directions.

`atan2(y, x)` sees both signs separately:

- $x>0$, $y>0$: quadrant I.
- $x<0$, $y>0$: quadrant II.
- $x<0$, $y<0$: quadrant III.
- $x>0$, $y<0$: quadrant IV.

That is why robotics, graphics, navigation, and control code usually prefer `atan2` for heading reconstruction.

## Domain And Range Checks

Arcsin and arccos only accept values from -1 to 1. That is because sine and cosine outputs are never outside that range.

If you calculate:

$$
\arccos(1.2)
$$

something upstream is wrong, or floating-point error pushed a value slightly outside range.

Practical code sometimes clamps tiny numerical drift:

```js
const clamped = Math.max(-1, Math.min(1, value));
```

But do not blindly clamp large errors. If a ratio is 1.2, the geometry may be impossible.

## Degrees, Radians, And Code

Programming inverse trig functions usually return radians.

In JavaScript:

```js
const angleRadians = Math.atan2(y, x);
const angleDegrees = angleRadians * 180 / Math.PI;
```

In Python:

```py
angle_radians = math.atan2(y, x)
angle_degrees = math.degrees(angle_radians)
```

Always label the output. A result of `0.785` is radians. A result of `45` is degrees.

## Solving Equations Versus Principal Values

There is a difference between:

$$
\arcsin(1/2)
$$

and:

$$
\sin\theta=1/2
$$

The first asks for the principal arcsin output:

$$
30^\circ
$$

The second asks for all angles that satisfy the equation:

$$
30^\circ,\ 150^\circ,\ 390^\circ,\ 510^\circ,\ ...
$$

For right-triangle engineering problems, the principal angle is often enough. For wave, rotation, and periodic equations, it is not.

## Engineering Connections

### Slope To Angle

If a ramp grade is 0.12, then:

$$
\theta=\arctan(0.12)\approx6.84^\circ
$$

This converts a design slope into an angle.

### Angle Error In Control

If a robot is offset by components $(x,y)$ from a target, `atan2(y,x)` gives the target bearing. Subtract the robot's current heading to get angular error.

### Sensor Tilt

If a camera is height $h$ and must hit the floor distance $d$ ahead:

$$
\theta=\arctan(h/d)
$$

This gives the required downward tilt angle.

### Dot Product Preview

Later, the angle between vectors can be recovered using:

$$
\theta=\arccos\left(\frac{a\cdot b}{|a||b|}\right)
$$

That is an arccos problem because the dot product measures the cosine of the angle between vectors.

## Common Failure Modes

1. **Reading $\sin^{-1}x$ as reciprocal sine.** It usually means arcsin.
2. **Expecting arcsin to return every possible angle.** It returns a principal value.
3. **Using arctan instead of `atan2` for signed components.**
4. **Forgetting radians from code output.**
5. **Feeding arcsin or arccos values outside -1 to 1.**
6. **Choosing arcsin when the known ratio is adjacent/hypotenuse.**
7. **Choosing arccos when the known ratio is opposite/adjacent.**
8. **Ignoring quadrant.**
9. **Treating inverse functions as undoing periodic trig globally.** They only undo on restricted ranges.
10. **Not checking physical plausibility.** If a cable's vertical rise exceeds its length, the ratio is impossible.
