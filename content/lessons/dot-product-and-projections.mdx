# Dot Product and Projections

## Re-entry Promise

This lesson assumes you can read a vector like $(3,4)$, compute magnitude, add and scale vectors, and use cosine for angles. It does not assume you already know why "multiplying" two vectors should mean anything.

Ordinary multiplication takes two numbers and returns one number. Vector multiplication is different because vectors carry direction. There are several useful products. The dot product is the one that answers this question:

> How much of one vector points in the direction of another?

That question appears everywhere in physical AI: forward speed along a path, force doing work, a camera ray aligned with a surface normal, a robot's error along a constraint, and a drone's wind component along its route.

## Objective

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- Compute the dot product from components in 2D and 3D.
- Explain why the result is a scalar, not a vector.
- Use the geometric formula $\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}=|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta$.
- Detect whether two nonzero vectors point mostly together, mostly opposite, or perpendicular.
- Use the dot product to compute the angle between vectors.
- Project one vector onto another vector.
- Distinguish scalar projection from vector projection.
- Interpret projections in engineering contexts: work, wind, navigation, sensor alignment, and control error.
- Spot common mistakes, especially projecting onto a non-unit vector incorrectly.
- Build a small projection calculator and verify it against worked examples.

## Plain-Language Intuition

Imagine shining a flashlight straight down onto the floor. Your hand casts a shadow. The shadow is not the full hand; it is the part of the hand that lies in the floor direction.

A projection is a vector shadow.

If a drone velocity points partly east and partly north, its eastward projection is "how much of that velocity is east." If a force pushes partly along a rail and partly sideways into the rail, the projection along the rail is the part that actually moves the cart. If a robot arm position error points partly along a joint's allowed motion and partly into a hard stop, the projection tells the controller which component can be corrected by that joint.

The dot product is the arithmetic tool that measures that shadow.

## Why This Idea Exists

Engineers needed a compact way to measure alignment. Magnitude alone cannot do it. Two 10 N forces can point in the same direction, opposite directions, or at right angles, and those cases have completely different physical effects.

The dot product solves this by combining size and angle into one scalar:

$$
\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}=|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta
$$

If the angle is small, $\cos\theta$ is positive and large, so the dot product is positive. If the vectors are perpendicular, $\cos 90^\circ=0$, so the dot product is zero. If they point more than 90 degrees apart, $\cos\theta$ is negative, so the dot product is negative.

That sign is not decoration. It tells you whether one vector helps, does nothing, or works against another.

## Core Theory

### Component Definition

For 2D vectors:

$$
\vec{a}=(a_x,a_y), \quad \vec{b}=(b_x,b_y)
$$

the dot product is:

$$
\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}=a_xb_x+a_yb_y
$$

For 3D vectors:

$$
\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}=a_xb_x+a_yb_y+a_zb_z
$$

In any dimension, multiply matching components and add the products.

### Geometric Definition

The same operation also equals:

$$
\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}=|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta
$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the vectors, always taken from 0 degrees to 180 degrees.

This formula is the meaning. The component formula is the fast computation.

The bridge between the component formula and the geometric formula comes from distance.

For two vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$, the vector between their heads is $\vec{a}-\vec{b}$. Its squared length is:

$$
|\vec{a}-\vec{b}|^2=(a_x-b_x)^2+(a_y-b_y)^2
$$

Expand:

$$
|\vec{a}-\vec{b}|^2=a_x^2+a_y^2+b_x^2+b_y^2-2(a_xb_x+a_yb_y)
$$

That is:

$$
|\vec{a}-\vec{b}|^2=|\vec{a}|^2+|\vec{b}|^2-2(\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b})
$$

But the law of cosines for the triangle formed by $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ says:

$$
|\vec{a}-\vec{b}|^2=|\vec{a}|^2+|\vec{b}|^2-2|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta
$$

Comparing the two equations gives:

$$
\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}=|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta
$$

So the component formula and the angle formula are the same fact written in two languages.

### Sign Meaning

For nonzero vectors:

- $\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}>0$: angle is less than 90 degrees; the vectors mostly point the same way.
- $\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}=0$: angle is 90 degrees; the vectors are perpendicular.
- $\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}<0$: angle is greater than 90 degrees; the vectors mostly point opposite ways.

### Why Perpendicular Gives Zero

If two vectors are perpendicular, one has no shadow on the other. A force pushing sideways on a sliding cart does no work along the direction of motion. A drone flying north has zero eastward velocity. The dot product records that absence with a zero.

### Magnitude From Dot Product

A vector dotted with itself gives squared magnitude:

$$
\vec{v}\cdot\vec{v}=v_x^2+v_y^2+v_z^2=|\vec{v}|^2
$$

So:

$$
|\vec{v}|=\sqrt{\vec{v}\cdot\vec{v}}
$$

This is one reason the dot product becomes central in linear algebra.

### Angle Between Vectors

Rearrange the geometric formula:

$$
\cos\theta=\frac{\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}}{|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|}
$$

Then:

$$
\theta=\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}}{|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|}\right)
$$

This only works when both vectors are nonzero. The zero vector has no direction, so it cannot have an angle with another vector.

### Scalar Projection

The scalar projection of $\vec{a}$ onto $\vec{b}$ is the signed length of $\vec{a}$ in the direction of $\vec{b}$:

$$
\operatorname{comp}_{\vec{b}}\vec{a}=\frac{\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}}{|\vec{b}|}
$$

It is a number. It can be positive, zero, or negative.

Think of scalar projection as shadow length. Shine a light perpendicular to $\vec{b}$ so that $\vec{a}$ casts a shadow onto the line of $\vec{b}$. The signed length of that shadow is:

$$
|\vec{a}|\cos\theta
$$

Using the dot product formula:

$$
\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}=|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta
$$

Divide by $|\vec{b}|$:

$$
\frac{\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}}{|\vec{b}|}=|\vec{a}|\cos\theta
$$

That is why the scalar projection formula works. It measures how much of $\vec{a}$ lies along $\vec{b}$, not how long $\vec{a}$ is overall.

### Vector Projection

The vector projection of $\vec{a}$ onto $\vec{b}$ is the actual vector shadow of $\vec{a}$ along $\vec{b}$:

$$
\operatorname{proj}_{\vec{b}}\vec{a}
=\left(\frac{\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}}{\vec{b}\cdot\vec{b}}\right)\vec{b}
$$

This version works even when $\vec{b}$ is not a unit vector, as long as $\vec{b}\ne\vec{0}$.

If $\hat{b}$ is a unit vector, then:

$$
\operatorname{proj}_{\hat{b}}\vec{a}=(\vec{a}\cdot\hat{b})\hat{b}
$$

The unit-vector form is beautiful, but it only applies after normalization.

## Visual Or Mental Model

Draw $\vec{b}$ as a floor line. Drop a perpendicular from the head of $\vec{a}$ down to that floor line. The arrow from the tail to the dropped point is $\operatorname{proj}_{\vec{b}}\vec{a}$.

Three cases matter:

1. If the dropped point lands in front of the tail along $\vec{b}$, the projection is positive.
2. If it lands exactly at the tail, the projection is zero.
3. If it lands behind the tail, the projection is negative.

This mental picture is the fastest way to avoid sign mistakes.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Component Dot Product

Compute:

$$
(2,3)\cdot(4,5)
$$

Multiply matching components:

$$
2\cdot4+3\cdot5=8+15=23
$$

The dot product is 23.

### Example 2: Perpendicular Test

Are $(3,4)$ and $(-4,3)$ perpendicular?

$$
(3,4)\cdot(-4,3)=3(-4)+4(3)=-12+12=0
$$

Yes. The dot product is zero, so the vectors are perpendicular.

### Example 3: Angle Between Vectors

Find the angle between $\vec{a}=(1,0)$ and $\vec{b}=(1,1)$.

$$
\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}=1
$$

$$
|\vec{a}|=1,\quad |\vec{b}|=\sqrt2
$$

$$
\cos\theta=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}
$$

So $\theta=45^\circ$.

### Example 4: Scalar Projection

Project $\vec{a}=(6,2)$ onto $\vec{b}=(3,0)$ as a scalar length.

$$
\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}=18
$$

$$
|\vec{b}|=3
$$

$$
\operatorname{comp}_{\vec{b}}\vec{a}=18/3=6
$$

The part of $\vec{a}$ along the positive $x$ direction is 6.

### Example 5: Vector Projection

Project $\vec{a}=(4,3)$ onto $\vec{b}=(1,0)$.

$$
\operatorname{proj}_{\vec{b}}\vec{a}
=\left(\frac{(4,3)\cdot(1,0)}{(1,0)\cdot(1,0)}\right)(1,0)
=4(1,0)=(4,0)
$$

The vector shadow is $(4,0)$.

### Example 6: Non-Unit Projection

Project $\vec{a}=(4,3)$ onto $\vec{b}=(2,0)$.

Do not use $(\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b})\vec{b}$; $\vec{b}$ is not unit length.

$$
\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}=8,\quad \vec{b}\cdot\vec{b}=4
$$

$$
\operatorname{proj}_{\vec{b}}\vec{a}=(8/4)(2,0)=(4,0)
$$

The projection is still $(4,0)$. The direction line is the same.

### Example 7: Work Done By A Force

A cart moves $\vec{d}=(5,0)$ m. A force $\vec{F}=(10,6)$ N acts on it. Work is:

$$
W=\vec{F}\cdot\vec{d}=10\cdot5+6\cdot0=50\text{ J}
$$

Only the force component along displacement contributes to work. The sideways 6 N component does no work along the cart's path.

### Example 8: Wind Along A Drone Path

A drone wants to fly along unit direction $\hat{p}=(0.6,0.8)$. Wind velocity is $\vec{w}=(5,0)$ m/s. The wind component along the path is:

$$
\vec{w}\cdot\hat{p}=5(0.6)+0(0.8)=3\text{ m/s}
$$

The wind gives a 3 m/s tailwind along the desired path.

## Engineering Connections

### Robotics Control

An error vector from robot to target may contain a component along a path and a component across the path. Projecting the error onto the path tangent tells the controller how far forward or backward it is. Projecting onto the path normal tells it how far sideways it has drifted.

### Computer Vision

Surface normals are often unit vectors. A lighting direction dotted with a surface normal measures how strongly that surface faces the light. This is the backbone of simple shading models and appears again in 3D perception.

### Physics

Work is a dot product:

$$
W=\vec{F}\cdot\vec{d}
$$

No alignment means no work. Opposite alignment means negative work, like braking.

### Drone Navigation

Wind, velocity, and route direction are vectors. Projection tells you headwind or tailwind:

$$
\text{wind along route}=\vec{w}\cdot\hat{r}
$$

The perpendicular component tells you crosswind.

### Sensor Alignment

If a camera optical axis and target direction have dot product near 1 after normalization, the camera is nearly aimed at the target. If it is near 0, the target is far off-axis.

## Common Failure Modes

1. Treating the dot product as component-wise multiplication instead of summing the products.
2. Forgetting the dot product is a scalar.
3. Using the angle formula with the zero vector.
4. Confusing scalar projection with vector projection.
5. Projecting onto a non-unit vector as if it were unit length.
6. Thinking dot product zero means one vector is zero. Nonzero perpendicular vectors also dot to zero.
7. Forgetting negative dot products are meaningful.
8. Mixing degrees and radians when computing inverse cosine in code.
9. Using the wrong vector as the projection target.
10. Dropping units: force dot displacement gives joules, not newtons or meters.
