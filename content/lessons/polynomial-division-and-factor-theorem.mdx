# Polynomial Division and the Factor Theorem

## Re-entry Promise

This lesson assumes you can factor friendly polynomials and multiply polynomials. It does not assume polynomial long division or synthetic division is in memory.

Factoring is powerful when the factors are visible. Polynomial division is what you use when they are not visible yet. It lets you test candidate roots, reduce a polynomial's degree, and rewrite rational expressions in more revealing forms.

By the end, you should be able to divide polynomials, use the remainder theorem, use the factor theorem, and explain why these tools matter for roots, models, and transfer functions.

## Objective

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- Divide polynomials using long division.
- Divide by a linear factor using synthetic division.
- Interpret quotient and remainder.
- State and use the remainder theorem.
- State and use the factor theorem.
- Use the rational root theorem to build a finite candidate list for integer/rational roots.
- Test whether $x-a$ is a factor of a polynomial.
- Reduce a polynomial after finding one root.
- Use division to rewrite improper rational expressions.
- Connect polynomial division to roots, system factors, calibration models, and transfer functions.

## Plain-Language Intuition

Polynomial division is ordinary division with powers of $x$.

When you divide 17 by 5, you get:

$$
17=5(3)+2
$$

When you divide a polynomial $P(x)$ by another polynomial $D(x)$, you get:

$$
P(x)=D(x)Q(x)+R(x)
$$

The quotient $Q(x)$ is the main result. The remainder $R(x)$ is what is left over.

If you divide by $x-a$ and the remainder is zero, then $x-a$ is a factor and $a$ is a root.

## Why This Idea Exists

Polynomial division gives structure when expanded form hides it:

- It turns one known root into a smaller polynomial to solve.
- It tests candidate roots without full factoring.
- It rewrites rational functions into polynomial plus remainder form.
- It reveals slant asymptotes.
- It appears in control systems when analyzing transfer functions.
- It supports numerical methods that find roots one at a time.

The key idea: division connects values, roots, and factors.

## Core Theory

### Division Algorithm

For polynomials $P(x)$ and $D(x)$ with $D(x)\ne0$:

$$
P(x)=D(x)Q(x)+R(x)
$$

where either $R(x)=0$ or the degree of $R$ is less than the degree of $D$.

This is the polynomial version of ordinary division.

For whole numbers:

$$
17=5(3)+2
$$

The remainder 2 is smaller than the divisor 5. If the remainder were 7, you could still pull out another 5, so the division would not be finished.

For polynomials, "smaller" means lower degree. If the remainder still has degree at least as large as the divisor, you can keep dividing. The algorithm stops only when the leftover part is too low-degree to contain another copy of the divisor.

Example:

$$
x^3+2x+1=(x+1)(x^2-x+3)-2
$$

Here the divisor has degree 1. The remainder $-2$ has degree 0, so the division is finished.

The division algorithm is what makes the later theorems possible. It says every division problem has a quotient part and a remainder part, and those parts are not arbitrary.

### Long Division

Divide:

$$
x^2+5x+6
$$

by:

$$
x+2
$$

Ask what multiplies $x$ to get $x^2$: answer $x$. Multiply:

$$
x(x+2)=x^2+2x
$$

Subtract:

$$
(x^2+5x+6)-(x^2+2x)=3x+6
$$

Ask what multiplies $x$ to get $3x$: answer 3. Multiply:

$$
3(x+2)=3x+6
$$

Subtract to get 0. Quotient:

$$
x+3
$$

The long-division process is not about memorizing a layout. It is repeated leading-term cancellation.

The leading term of the dividend is $x^2$. The leading term of the divisor is $x$. To cancel $x^2$, you need $x$ copies of the divisor:

$$
x(x+2)=x^2+2x
$$

After subtracting, the new leading term is $3x$. To cancel $3x$, you need 3 more copies:

$$
3(x+2)=3x+6
$$

So the quotient is $x+3$ because the dividend contains $x$ copies of $(x+2)$ plus 3 more copies of $(x+2)$.

Check by multiplying:

$$
(x+2)(x+3)=x^2+5x+6
$$

This check matters. Division and multiplication are inverse operations, so every polynomial division result should be verified by:

$$
\text{divisor}\cdot\text{quotient}+\text{remainder}=\text{original dividend}
$$

### Synthetic Division

Synthetic division is a shortcut for dividing by $x-a$.

For:

$$
x^2+5x+6
$$

divided by $x+2$, use $a=-2$. Coefficients:

$$
1,\ 5,\ 6
$$

Bring down 1, multiply by -2, add, repeat:

$$
1,\ 3,\ 0
$$

Quotient coefficients are $1,3$, so quotient is $x+3$, remainder 0.

### Why Synthetic Division Works

Synthetic division is not a separate kind of algebra. It is long division with the repeated powers of $x$ hidden so you can focus on coefficients.

When dividing by $x-a$, every step asks:

1. What leading term cancels the current leading term?
2. What new coefficient remains after subtracting?
3. How does multiplication by $a$ affect the next coefficient?

The compact table works only because the divisor is linear and has leading coefficient 1. If you are dividing by $2x-3$, ordinary synthetic division does not apply directly unless you rewrite the root as $x-\frac32$ and handle the scale carefully. When in doubt, use long division.

### Remainder Theorem

When $P(x)$ is divided by $x-a$, the remainder is:

$$
P(a)
$$

Here is why.

The division algorithm says:

$$
P(x)=(x-a)Q(x)+R
$$

Because the divisor $x-a$ has degree 1, the remainder must have degree less than 1. That means the remainder is just a constant, so write it as $R$.

Now evaluate both sides at $x=a$:

$$
P(a)=(a-a)Q(a)+R
$$

The factor $(a-a)$ is zero:

$$
P(a)=0\cdot Q(a)+R
$$

So:

$$
P(a)=R
$$

That is the remainder theorem. Evaluation at $a$ secretly performs the final remainder check after division by $x-a$.

Example:

$$
P(x)=x^2+5x+6
$$

Divide by $x+2=x-(-2)$. Remainder is:

$$
P(-2)=4-10+6=0
$$

### Factor Theorem

$x-a$ is a factor of $P(x)$ if and only if:

$$
P(a)=0
$$

This is the root-factor connection.

This follows immediately from the remainder theorem.

If $P(a)=0$, then the remainder after division by $x-a$ is zero:

$$
P(x)=(x-a)Q(x)+0
$$

So:

$$
P(x)=(x-a)Q(x)
$$

That means $x-a$ is a factor.

In the other direction, if $x-a$ is already a factor, then:

$$
P(x)=(x-a)Q(x)
$$

Evaluate at $x=a$:

$$
P(a)=(a-a)Q(a)=0
$$

So a factor creates a root, and a root creates a factor. This is one of the central bridges in algebra: graph crossings, numerical evaluations, and symbolic factors are three views of the same event.

### Rational Root Theorem

The rational root theorem gives a finite list of rational roots worth testing.

For a polynomial with integer coefficients:

$$
P(x)=a_nx^n+\cdots+a_1x+a_0
$$

any rational root $\frac{p}{q}$ in lowest terms must have:

- $p$ dividing the constant term $a_0$,
- $q$ dividing the leading coefficient $a_n$.

Example:

$$
P(x)=2x^3-3x^2-8x+12
$$

Constant term is 12. Leading coefficient is 2.

Possible rational roots:

$$
\pm1,\pm2,\pm3,\pm4,\pm6,\pm12,\pm\frac12,\pm\frac32
$$

This theorem does not guarantee that every candidate works. It only says every rational root must be on the list. You still test candidates with evaluation or synthetic division.

Why this matters: without the theorem, guessing roots is open-ended. With it, root hunting becomes a bounded procedure.

### Reducing Degree

If $P(x)$ has root $a$, divide by $x-a$ to reduce its degree.

Example:

$$
P(x)=x^3-6x^2+11x-6
$$

Test $x=1$:

$$
P(1)=1-6+11-6=0
$$

So $x-1$ is a factor. Dividing gives:

$$
x^2-5x+6
$$

Then:

$$
x^2-5x+6=(x-2)(x-3)
$$

So:

$$
P(x)=(x-1)(x-2)(x-3)
$$

## Visual Or Mental Model

Think of a polynomial as a composite machine. A root tells you one removable module: $x-a$. Division removes that module and exposes the remaining machine.

The remainder is a diagnostic. Remainder zero means exact fit. Nonzero remainder means the candidate was not a factor.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Long Division

Divide:

$$
x^3+2x^2-5x-6
$$

by:

$$
x+1
$$

Synthetic division with $a=-1$ and coefficients $1,2,-5,-6$:

Bring down 1. Multiply by -1, add to 2 -> 1. Multiply by -1, add to -5 -> -6. Multiply by -1, add to -6 -> 0.

Quotient:

$$
x^2+x-6
$$

Remainder 0.

### Example 2: Remainder Theorem

Find the remainder when:

$$
P(x)=2x^3-x+4
$$

is divided by $x-2$.

Remainder:

$$
P(2)=2(8)-2+4=18
$$

### Example 3: Factor Theorem

Is $x-3$ a factor of:

$$
P(x)=x^3-4x^2+x+6?
$$

Compute:

$$
P(3)=27-36+3+6=0
$$

Yes.

### Example 4: Find All Roots After One Root

For:

$$
P(x)=x^3-4x^2+x+6
$$

we know $x=3$ is a root. Divide by $x-3$ to get:

$$
x^2-x-2
$$

Factor:

$$
x^2-x-2=(x-2)(x+1)
$$

Roots:

$$
3,\ 2,\ -1
$$

### Example 4B: Rational Root Theorem In Use

Find rational roots of:

$$
P(x)=2x^3-3x^2-8x+12
$$

Possible rational roots:

$$
\pm1,\pm2,\pm3,\pm4,\pm6,\pm12,\pm\frac12,\pm\frac32
$$

Test $x=2$:

$$
P(2)=16-12-16+12=0
$$

So $x-2$ is a factor. Synthetic division by 2 gives quotient:

$$
2x^2+x-6
$$

Factor:

$$
2x^2+x-6=(2x-3)(x+2)
$$

So:

$$
P(x)=(x-2)(2x-3)(x+2)
$$

Roots:

$$
x=2,\quad x=\frac32,\quad x=-2
$$

Notice how the theorem turned a cubic into a finite search, and division reduced it to a quadratic.

### Example 5: Improper Rational Expression

Divide:

$$
\frac{x^2+3x+5}{x+1}
$$

Long division gives quotient $x+2$, remainder 3:

$$
\frac{x^2+3x+5}{x+1}=x+2+\frac{3}{x+1}
$$

This form reveals the slant asymptote $y=x+2$.

### Example 6: Missing Terms

Divide:

$$
x^3+1
$$

by $x+1$. Include missing terms:

$$
x^3+0x^2+0x+1
$$

Synthetic division by -1 gives quotient:

$$
x^2-x+1
$$

remainder 0.

### Example 7: Candidate Not A Factor

For:

$$
P(x)=x^3+x+1
$$

test $x=1$:

$$
P(1)=3
$$

So $x-1$ is not a factor.

### Example 8: Control-Style Interpretation

A transfer-function denominator might be:

$$
s^3+6s^2+11s+6
$$

Factoring reveals:

$$
(s+1)(s+2)(s+3)
$$

The factors expose poles at $s=-1,-2,-3$, which strongly influence system response.

### Example 9: Failure Mode From A Missing Zero Coefficient

Divide:

$$
P(x)=x^4-3x+2
$$

by $x-1$.

The coefficient list is not:

$$
1,\ -3,\ 2
$$

That would describe $x^2-3x+2$, a completely different polynomial.

The correct list is:

$$
1,\ 0,\ 0,\ -3,\ 2
$$

for:

$$
x^4+0x^3+0x^2-3x+2
$$

Synthetic division by 1 gives quotient coefficients:

$$
1,\ 1,\ 1,\ -2
$$

and remainder 0. So:

$$
x^4-3x+2=(x-1)(x^3+x^2+x-2)
$$

The placeholder zeros are not bookkeeping decoration. They preserve the power structure.

## Engineering Connections

### Control Systems

Denominator factors reveal poles. Numerator factors reveal zeros. Division and factor testing help uncover both.

### Signal Processing

Filter transfer functions are rational expressions. Polynomial division and factoring help analyze long-term behavior and stability.

### Numerical Modeling

If a model has a known root or boundary condition, division reduces complexity.

### Calibration

Polynomial calibration models can be tested for known zeros or offsets.

### Robotics Trajectories

Boundary-time factors like $t$ or $(t-T)$ ensure a trajectory has specified zero values at start or finish.

## Common Failure Modes

1. Forgetting placeholder zeros for missing powers.
2. Using synthetic division with the wrong sign.
3. Confusing divisor $x+2$ with $a=2$ instead of $a=-2$.
4. Forgetting the remainder.
5. Thinking nonzero remainder means division failed.
6. Applying the factor theorem to divisors not of form $x-a$ without rewriting.
7. Dropping degree after division incorrectly.
8. Not checking by multiplication.
9. Treating a root as a factor directly instead of $x-a$.
10. Forgetting physical constraints after finding roots.
