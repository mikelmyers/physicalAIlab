# Polynomial Functions and End Behavior

## Re-entry Promise

This lesson assumes you can evaluate functions, understand exponents with whole-number powers, and read a coordinate graph. It does not assume you remember polynomial vocabulary from school.

Polynomials are built from powers of $x$ added together. They are some of the most useful functions in engineering because they are smooth, easy to compute, and good local approximations to more complicated behavior.

By the end, you should be able to identify polynomial degree, leading coefficient, roots, turning behavior, and end behavior from a formula or graph.

## Objective

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- Define a polynomial function.
- Identify terms, coefficients, degree, constant term, and leading coefficient.
- Classify constant, linear, quadratic, cubic, quartic, and higher-degree polynomials.
- Evaluate polynomial functions.
- Explain end behavior from degree and leading coefficient.
- Recognize roots/zeros and intercepts.
- Understand multiplicity at an introductory level.
- Explain why a degree-$n$ polynomial has at most $n-1$ turning points.
- Connect polynomial models to calibration curves, trajectory approximations, and local linear/quadratic models.
- Build a small polynomial evaluator and end-behavior classifier.

## Plain-Language Intuition

A polynomial is a weighted sum of powers:

$$
p(x)=a_nx^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1x+a_0
$$

The powers shape the curve. The coefficients decide how much of each shape is mixed in.

For small $x$, several terms may matter. For very large positive or negative $x$, the highest-power term dominates. That is why the leading term controls end behavior.

If:

$$
p(x)=2x^5-100x^2+7
$$

then for huge $|x|$, $2x^5$ eventually overwhelms $-100x^2$ and 7. End behavior listens to the leading term.

## Why This Idea Exists

Polynomials are useful because they are algebraically simple and computationally cheap:

- Lines approximate behavior near an operating point.
- Quadratics model acceleration, parabolic reflectors, and simple energy bowls.
- Cubics and quintics create smooth robot trajectories.
- Polynomial calibration curves map sensor readings to physical values.
- Taylor polynomials approximate complex functions in calculus.
- Least-squares fitting often starts with polynomial models.

Polynomials are not the whole world, but they are one of the first serious model families you must master.

## Core Theory

### Polynomial Definition

A polynomial in $x$ has the form:

$$
p(x)=a_nx^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1x+a_0
$$

where:

- exponents are nonnegative integers,
- coefficients are real numbers in this course,
- $a_n\ne0$ for the highest-degree term.

Examples:

$$
3x^4-2x^2+7
$$

$$
-5x+9
$$

$$
12
$$

Non-examples:

$$
x^{-1},\quad \sqrt{x},\quad 2^x
$$

These are useful functions, but not polynomials.

### Vocabulary

For:

$$
p(x)=4x^3-2x^2+7x-9
$$

- Terms: $4x^3$, $-2x^2$, $7x$, $-9$.
- Coefficients: 4, -2, 7, -9.
- Degree: 3.
- Leading term: $4x^3$.
- Leading coefficient: 4.
- Constant term: -9.

### Classification By Degree

- Degree 0: constant.
- Degree 1: linear.
- Degree 2: quadratic.
- Degree 3: cubic.
- Degree 4: quartic.
- Degree 5: quintic.

### Evaluating A Polynomial

Let:

$$
p(x)=2x^3-3x+1
$$

Then:

$$
p(2)=2(2^3)-3(2)+1=16-6+1=11
$$

### End Behavior

End behavior asks what happens as $x\to\infty$ and $x\to-\infty$.

Only the leading term matters.

#### Even Degree, Positive Leading Coefficient

Example:

$$
p(x)=x^4-3x+1
$$

Both ends go up:

$$
p(x)\to\infty \text{ as } x\to\infty
$$

$$
p(x)\to\infty \text{ as } x\to-\infty
$$

#### Even Degree, Negative Leading Coefficient

Example:

$$
p(x)=-x^4+2x
$$

Both ends go down.

#### Odd Degree, Positive Leading Coefficient

Example:

$$
p(x)=x^3-5x
$$

Left end down, right end up.

#### Odd Degree, Negative Leading Coefficient

Example:

$$
p(x)=-x^3+5x
$$

Left end up, right end down.

### Roots And Zeros

A root or zero is an input where:

$$
p(x)=0
$$

On the graph, real roots are $x$-intercepts.

If:

$$
p(x)=(x-2)(x+3)
$$

then roots are:

$$
x=2,\quad x=-3
$$

### Multiplicity

If a factor repeats, the root has multiplicity greater than 1.

Example:

$$
p(x)=(x-2)^2(x+1)
$$

Root $x=2$ has multiplicity 2. Root $x=-1$ has multiplicity 1.

Introductory graph rule:

- Odd multiplicity roots usually cross the axis.
- Even multiplicity roots usually touch and turn around.

### Turning Points

A degree-$n$ polynomial has at most $n-1$ turning points.

This is not saying it always has that many. A cubic can have 0 or 2 turning points. A quartic can have up to 3.

Calculus will explain this through derivatives. For now, treat it as a shape constraint.

## Visual Or Mental Model

Think of the leading term as the engine that controls the far left and far right. Lower-degree terms can bend the graph in the middle, shift roots, and create turns, but they lose the long-distance tug-of-war.

For end behavior:

1. Look only at the leading term.
2. Ask whether degree is even or odd.
3. Ask whether leading coefficient is positive or negative.
4. Decide left end and right end.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Identify Degree And Leading Coefficient

For:

$$
p(x)=-3x^5+2x^2-7
$$

Degree is 5. Leading coefficient is -3. Constant term is -7.

### Example 2: Polynomial Or Not

$$
f(x)=4x^3-x+1
$$

is a polynomial.

$$
g(x)=x^{-2}+1
$$

is not a polynomial because exponent -2 is not a nonnegative integer.

### Example 3: Evaluate

Let:

$$
p(x)=x^4-2x^2+3
$$

Find $p(-2)$.

$$
p(-2)=(-2)^4-2(-2)^2+3=16-8+3=11
$$

### Example 4: End Behavior

Let:

$$
p(x)=-2x^6+5x^3-1
$$

Leading term is $-2x^6$. Degree is even, leading coefficient is negative. Both ends go down.

### Example 5: Odd Positive End Behavior

Let:

$$
p(x)=3x^5-100x^2+1
$$

Leading term is $3x^5$. Odd degree, positive leading coefficient. Left end down, right end up.

### Example 6: Roots From Factored Form

Let:

$$
p(x)=2(x-4)(x+1)(x-3)
$$

Roots:

$$
x=4,\quad x=-1,\quad x=3
$$

The factor 2 changes vertical scale but not root locations.

### Example 7: Multiplicity

Let:

$$
p(x)=(x+2)^2(x-5)
$$

Root $x=-2$ has multiplicity 2, so the graph touches/bounces there. Root $x=5$ has multiplicity 1, so the graph crosses there.

### Example 8: Calibration Curve

A sensor calibration model is:

$$
d(v)=0.8v^2+1.5v+0.2
$$

where $v$ is voltage and $d$ is distance in meters.

At $v=2$:

$$
d(2)=0.8(4)+1.5(2)+0.2=3.2+3+0.2=6.4
$$

The polynomial turns a voltage reading into an estimated physical distance.

## Engineering Connections

### Sensor Calibration

Real sensors are often nonlinear. Polynomial fits can approximate voltage-to-distance, temperature-to-resistance, or pixel-to-world mappings over a limited range.

### Robot Trajectories

Smooth trajectories often use cubic or quintic polynomials so position, velocity, and acceleration behave well at endpoints.

### Physics Approximation

Near a stable point, many energy functions look quadratic. That is why springs and small oscillations show up everywhere.

### Control And Optimization

Quadratic cost functions penalize error smoothly and are easy to optimize. Polynomials also appear in characteristic equations.

### Computer Vision

Camera calibration and distortion correction can use polynomial terms to model lens effects.

## Common Failure Modes

1. Calling $x^{-1}$ a polynomial.
2. Calling $\sqrt{x}$ a polynomial.
3. Choosing degree from the first written term rather than the highest power.
4. Forgetting that a constant nonzero polynomial has degree 0.
5. Misreading a negative leading coefficient.
6. Using all terms for end behavior instead of the leading term.
7. Saying even degree always goes up; sign matters.
8. Saying odd degree always rises right; sign matters.
9. Confusing roots with coefficients.
10. Assuming degree $n$ means exactly $n$ real roots.
