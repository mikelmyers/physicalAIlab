# Quadratic Functions and the Quadratic Formula

## Re-entry Promise

This lesson assumes you can evaluate functions, multiply binomials, and read a graph. It does not assume you remember completing the square or the quadratic formula.

A quadratic is a degree-2 polynomial. It is the first function family where curvature becomes unavoidable. Lines have constant slope. Quadratics bend. That bending is why quadratics show up in projectile motion, area optimization, braking distance, energy wells, parabolic reflectors, and local approximations to complicated systems.

By the end, a quadratic should feel like an object you can inspect from several angles: standard form, vertex form, factored form, graph, roots, axis of symmetry, and discriminant.

## Objective

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- Recognize quadratic functions in standard form $ax^2+bx+c$.
- Identify $a$, $b$, and $c$.
- Explain why $a\ne0$.
- Find the vertex using $x=-b/(2a)$.
- Interpret the axis of symmetry.
- Convert simple quadratics between standard, factored, and vertex form.
- Solve quadratics by factoring when possible.
- Complete the square for a quadratic.
- Use the quadratic formula.
- Interpret the discriminant.
- Connect quadratics to projectile motion, braking distance, area, and energy.

## Plain-Language Intuition

A quadratic is a smooth U-shape or upside-down U-shape called a parabola.

If the coefficient of $x^2$ is positive, the parabola opens up. If it is negative, it opens down. The lowest or highest point is the vertex. The vertical line through the vertex is the axis of symmetry.

The roots are where the graph hits the $x$-axis. Some quadratics hit twice, some touch once, and some never hit the real $x$-axis.

The quadratic formula is a universal root-finder for degree 2. It is not magic. It comes from completing the square.

## Why This Idea Exists

Quadratics are the first serious nonlinear model:

- Constant acceleration motion gives position functions like $s(t)=s_0+v_0t+\frac12at^2$.
- Braking distance grows roughly with speed squared.
- Rectangular area with fixed perimeter is quadratic.
- Springs and small oscillations are modeled by quadratic energy near equilibrium.
- Reflectors use parabolic geometry to focus rays.
- Optimization often begins with a quadratic approximation.

If you can read quadratics well, calculus will feel less alien because many derivative and optimization examples start here.

## Core Theory

### Standard Form

A quadratic function has standard form:

$$
f(x)=ax^2+bx+c
$$

where $a$, $b$, and $c$ are constants and $a\ne0$.

If $a=0$, the $x^2$ term disappears and the function is linear, not quadratic.

### Opening Direction

- If $a>0$, the parabola opens upward.
- If $a<0$, the parabola opens downward.
- Larger $|a|$ makes the parabola narrower.
- Smaller $|a|$ makes it wider.

### Vertex And Axis

The vertex $x$-coordinate is:

$$
x_v=-\frac{b}{2a}
$$

Then:

$$
y_v=f(x_v)
$$

The axis of symmetry is:

$$
x=x_v
$$

### Factored Form

If a quadratic has roots $r_1$ and $r_2$, it can be written:

$$
f(x)=a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)
$$

This form makes roots visible.

Example:

$$
f(x)=2(x-3)(x+1)
$$

Roots: $x=3$ and $x=-1$.

### Vertex Form

Vertex form is:

$$
f(x)=a(x-h)^2+k
$$

The vertex is:

$$
(h,k)
$$

This form makes the graph's turning point visible.

### Completing The Square

Completing the square rewrites a quadratic so a squared binomial appears.

Example:

$$
x^2+6x+5
$$

Take half of 6, square it:

$$
(6/2)^2=9
$$

Add and subtract 9:

$$
x^2+6x+9-9+5=(x+3)^2-4
$$

Vertex: $(-3,-4)$.

### Quadratic Formula

For:

$$
ax^2+bx+c=0
$$

the solutions are:

$$
x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}
$$

The expression under the square root is the discriminant:

$$
\Delta=b^2-4ac
$$

### Discriminant Meaning

- $\Delta>0$: two distinct real roots.
- $\Delta=0$: one repeated real root.
- $\Delta<0$: no real roots; two complex roots.

The discriminant tells you what kind of intersection the parabola has with the $x$-axis.

## Visual Or Mental Model

Picture a parabola as a thrown ball's height over time, or as a bowl.

- Upward opening: bowl, minimum, stable energy shape.
- Downward opening: projectile arc, maximum height.
- Roots: ground hits or zero-crossings.
- Vertex: best/worst point.
- Axis: mirror line through the vertex.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Identify Coefficients

For:

$$
f(x)=3x^2-5x+2
$$

$a=3$, $b=-5$, $c=2$.

### Example 2: Vertex

Find the vertex of:

$$
f(x)=x^2-6x+8
$$

$$
x_v=-\frac{-6}{2(1)}=3
$$

$$
f(3)=9-18+8=-1
$$

Vertex: $(3,-1)$.

### Example 3: Factored Roots

Solve:

$$
(x-4)(x+2)=0
$$

Set each factor to zero:

$$
x=4,\quad x=-2
$$

### Example 4: Quadratic Formula

Solve:

$$
2x^2+3x-2=0
$$

Here $a=2$, $b=3$, $c=-2$.

$$
\Delta=3^2-4(2)(-2)=9+16=25
$$

$$
x=\frac{-3\pm5}{4}
$$

Roots:

$$
x=\frac12,\quad x=-2
$$

### Example 5: Discriminant

For:

$$
x^2+2x+5=0
$$

$$
\Delta=2^2-4(1)(5)=4-20=-16
$$

No real roots.

### Example 6: Projectile Height

Height:

$$
h(t)=-5t^2+20t+1
$$

The maximum occurs at:

$$
t_v=-\frac{20}{2(-5)}=2
$$

Height:

$$
h(2)=-20+40+1=21
$$

Maximum height is 21 m at 2 s.

### Example 7: Completing The Square

Rewrite:

$$
x^2+8x+10
$$

Half of 8 is 4, square is 16:

$$
x^2+8x+16-16+10=(x+4)^2-6
$$

Vertex: $(-4,-6)$.

### Example 8: Area Optimization

A rectangle has perimeter 20, so if one side is $x$, the other is $10-x$. Area:

$$
A(x)=x(10-x)=10x-x^2
$$

This opens downward. Vertex:

$$
x=-\frac{10}{2(-1)}=5
$$

Maximum area occurs at $x=5$, a square.

## Engineering Connections

### Projectile Motion

Vertical position under constant gravity is quadratic in time.

### Braking Distance

Kinetic energy depends on $v^2$, so stopping distance often scales with speed squared.

### Optimization

Many simple maximum/minimum problems reduce to a quadratic.

### Reflectors

Parabolas focus parallel rays to a point, which matters in antennas, lights, and optics.

### Energy Near Equilibrium

Stable systems often look quadratic near their minimum energy point.

## Common Failure Modes

1. Forgetting $a$ can be negative.
2. Using $-b/2a$ without parentheses.
3. Thinking the vertex $x$-coordinate is the root.
4. Mixing up standard, factored, and vertex form.
5. Forgetting the $\pm$ in the quadratic formula.
6. Losing a sign inside the discriminant.
7. Taking square root before computing all of $b^2-4ac$.
8. Assuming every quadratic has two real roots.
9. Forgetting that $\Delta=0$ means one repeated root.
10. Treating projectile maximum as a root instead of a vertex.
