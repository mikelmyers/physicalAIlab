# Bearings and Headings for Navigation

## Re-entry Promise

This lesson assumes you can read angles, use sine and cosine, and work with vectors in component form. It does not assume that compass bearings feel natural.

Most math lessons measure angles counter-clockwise from the positive $x$-axis. Most navigation systems measure bearings clockwise from North. That mismatch is small enough to look harmless and large enough to send a drone the wrong way.

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to move between compass bearings, math angles, east/north components, and vector headings without guessing.

## Objective

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- Define heading, bearing, course, and track in practical navigation language.
- Explain the difference between compass-angle convention and math-angle convention.
- Convert compass bearings to math angles.
- Convert math angles to compass bearings.
- Convert a distance and bearing into east/north displacement components.
- Recover a bearing from east/north displacement components using `atan2`.
- Distinguish desired course from actual track when wind or drift is present.
- Use vectors to combine airspeed, wind, and ground velocity.
- Recognize quadrant mistakes before they become navigation mistakes.
- Build a small bearing/vector navigation calculator.

## Plain-Language Intuition

Stand on a map. North is up, east is right.

A compass bearing tells you how far to turn clockwise from North. A bearing of 0 degrees is North. A bearing of 90 degrees is East. A bearing of 180 degrees is South. A bearing of 270 degrees is West.

Now stand on a math graph. The positive $x$-axis points right. Angles usually start at positive $x$ and turn counter-clockwise. A math angle of 0 degrees is East. A math angle of 90 degrees is North.

Both systems are useful. The problem is that they start from different axes and turn in opposite directions.

Navigation asks: "Which way on the Earth or map?" Math asks: "What angle from the $x$-axis?" Robotics and drones need both.

## Why This Idea Exists

Compasses came from navigation long before analytic geometry became standard engineering language. Navigators cared about North because Earth gives us a magnetic and geographic reference. Mathematicians cared about the $x$-axis because coordinate planes, trigonometric functions, and algebra become cleaner that way.

Physical AI systems live in both worlds:

- GPS and maps often speak latitude, longitude, North, East, and bearing.
- Robot planners often speak $x$, $y$, vectors, and angles from a coordinate frame.
- Autopilots distinguish commanded heading, actual track, wind correction, and yaw.
- GIS tools often use eastings and northings.
- Simulation engines often use their own coordinate conventions.

The skill is not memorizing one convention. The skill is naming the convention before computing.

## Core Theory

### Definitions

A **bearing** is an angle measured clockwise from North.

A **heading** is the direction a vehicle's nose is pointing.

A **course** is the desired path direction over the ground.

A **track** is the actual path direction over the ground.

In calm conditions, heading, course, and track can match. With wind, current, wheel slip, or control error, they may differ.

### Coordinate Convention

In this lesson:

- East is positive $x$.
- North is positive $y$.
- A displacement vector is $(E,N)$, meaning east component and north component.
- Compass bearing $B$ is measured clockwise from North.
- Math angle $\theta$ is measured counter-clockwise from positive East.

### Bearing To Math Angle

The conversion is:

$$
\theta = (90^\circ - B) \bmod 360^\circ
$$

Examples:

- Bearing 0 degrees -> math angle 90 degrees.
- Bearing 90 degrees -> math angle 0 degrees.
- Bearing 180 degrees -> math angle 270 degrees.
- Bearing 270 degrees -> math angle 180 degrees.

### Math Angle To Bearing

The inverse conversion is:

$$
B = (90^\circ - \theta) \bmod 360^\circ
$$

It looks the same because the transformation is its own inverse when wrapped modulo 360.

### Distance And Bearing To Components

If distance is $d$ and compass bearing is $B$, then:

$$
E = d\sin B
$$

$$
N = d\cos B
$$

This surprises many learners because math-angle components use cosine for $x$ and sine for $y$. Bearings are measured from North, so cosine lands on the North component and sine lands on the East component.

### Components To Bearing

Given east/north displacement $(E,N)$, use:

$$
B = \operatorname{atan2}(E,N)
$$

Then convert the result to degrees and wrap it into $0^\circ$ to $360^\circ$.

Notice the argument order: `atan2(E, N)`, not `atan2(N, E)`, because bearing is measured from North.

That argument order is the whole convention in one line.

For a math angle, zero points east and the usual formula is:

$$
\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)
$$

For a bearing, zero points north. If displacement is stored as $(E,N)$, the north component plays the role that $x$ normally plays in math-angle thinking, and the east component plays the role that $y$ normally plays. So:

$$
B=\operatorname{atan2}(E,N)
$$

This is not arbitrary. It is the same angle-reconstruction idea rotated so that North is zero.

### Ground Velocity With Wind

Velocity vectors add:

$$
\vec{v}_{ground}=\vec{v}_{air}+\vec{v}_{wind}
$$

If a drone points its nose at one heading, but wind blows sideways, the actual ground track changes. The heading is where the nose points; the track is where the drone actually moves over the ground.

The difference matters because the airframe does not move through still air and over the ground in the same way when wind exists. The motors produce air-relative velocity. GPS measures ground-relative velocity. Wind is the vector difference between those two stories:

$$
\vec{v}_{wind}=\vec{v}_{ground}-\vec{v}_{air}
$$

If you confuse heading with track, you may command the drone correctly but interpret the motion incorrectly.

## Visual Or Mental Model

Draw a compass rose on top of a coordinate plane:

- North sits on positive $y$.
- East sits on positive $x$.
- Compass bearings turn clockwise.
- Math angles turn counter-clockwise.

For a bearing, imagine starting at North and turning right. For a math angle, imagine starting at East and turning left.

That mental overlay prevents almost every bearing conversion mistake.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Bearing To Math Angle

Convert bearing 45 degrees to a math angle.

$$
\theta = 90^\circ - 45^\circ = 45^\circ
$$

Northeast happens to be 45 degrees in both systems.

### Example 2: Bearing 120 Degrees

Convert bearing 120 degrees to a math angle.

$$
\theta = 90^\circ - 120^\circ = -30^\circ
$$

Wrap into $0^\circ$ to $360^\circ$:

$$
330^\circ
$$

Bearing 120 degrees points southeast. A math angle of 330 degrees also points southeast.

### Example 3: Components From Bearing

A rover travels 100 m on bearing 30 degrees. Find east and north displacement.

$$
E = 100\sin 30^\circ=50\text{ m}
$$

$$
N = 100\cos 30^\circ\approx86.6\text{ m}
$$

Displacement is approximately $(50,\ 86.6)$ m.

### Example 4: Bearing From Components

A drone displacement is $(E,N)=(80,60)$ m. Find bearing.

$$
B=\operatorname{atan2}(80,60)
$$

$$
B\approx53.1^\circ
$$

The drone moved northeast, more east than north.

### Example 5: Westward Motion

A displacement is $(-40,0)$ m. East component is negative, north component is zero. The bearing is:

$$
B=\operatorname{atan2}(-40,0)=270^\circ
$$

The displacement is due west.

### Example 6: Wind Changes Track

A drone air velocity is 10 m/s due north:

$$
\vec{v}_{air}=(0,10)
$$

Wind is 3 m/s east:

$$
\vec{v}_{wind}=(3,0)
$$

Ground velocity:

$$
\vec{v}_{ground}=(3,10)
$$

Ground speed:

$$
\sqrt{3^2+10^2}\approx10.44\text{ m/s}
$$

Track bearing:

$$
\operatorname{atan2}(3,10)\approx16.7^\circ
$$

The drone points north but tracks 16.7 degrees east of north.

### Example 7: Crosswind And Along-Route Wind

A route direction is bearing 0 degrees, so the unit direction is $(0,1)$. Wind is $(4,3)$ m/s.

Along-route wind:

$$
(4,3)\cdot(0,1)=3\text{ m/s}
$$

The route gets a 3 m/s tailwind. The east component 4 m/s is crosswind.

### Example 8: Quadrant Failure

Suppose displacement is $(-50,-50)$ m. A careless calculation might use:

$$
\arctan(E/N)=\arctan(1)=45^\circ
$$

That is wrong because the displacement is southwest. `atan2(E,N)` sees both signs and gives 225 degrees.

## Engineering Connections

### Drone Autopilot

Autopilots often command heading, observe GPS track, and estimate wind. The difference between heading and track reveals drift. Vector decomposition turns that drift into along-track and cross-track error.

### Mobile Robots

A robot on a warehouse floor may use $x,y$ coordinates internally, while a human operator thinks in compass or building directions. Clear heading conversion prevents wrong-way commands.

### GIS And Field Surveying

Maps and GPS workflows often describe movement in eastings and northings. Bearing formulas convert between human-readable direction and machine-readable components.

### Marine And Air Navigation

Course over ground differs from heading when current or wind pushes the vehicle sideways. Vector addition is the honest model.

### Simulation

Simulators may define axes differently. Before using a heading formula, always identify which axis is forward, which is right, and whether rotations are clockwise or counter-clockwise.

## Common Failure Modes

1. Treating compass bearing as a math angle.
2. Using $E=d\cos B$ and $N=d\sin B$ for compass bearings.
3. Calling `atan2(N, E)` when computing a bearing.
4. Forgetting to wrap negative angles into $0^\circ$ to $360^\circ$.
5. Confusing heading with track.
6. Ignoring wind or drift when interpreting actual motion.
7. Mixing degrees and radians in code.
8. Forgetting that East is positive and West is negative.
9. Forgetting that North is positive and South is negative.
10. Assuming every software package uses the same frame convention.
