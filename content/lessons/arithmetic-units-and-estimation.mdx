# Arithmetic, Units, and Estimation

## Re-entry Promise

This lesson assumes you may not remember school math fluently. That is fine. We are going to rebuild number sense from the ground up because every later topic depends on it: algebra, circuits, code, robotics, drones, physics, simulations, and research.

You do not need to be fast yet. You need to become careful, honest, and repeatable.

## Objective

By the end, you should be able to:

- Treat numbers as measured quantities, not just symbols.
- Track units through simple calculations.
- Estimate the expected size of an answer before calculating.
- Catch obvious mistakes with reasonableness checks.
- Explain why arithmetic is part of engineering thinking, not just schoolwork.

## Why It Matters

A person can understand advanced ideas and still make beginner mistakes if their arithmetic, unit handling, and estimation are weak. In real technical work, those mistakes are not harmless.

A misplaced decimal can make:

- a resistor run too hot,
- a battery estimate wrong by hours,
- a drone flight plan unsafe,
- a telemetry chart misleading,
- a physics simulation meaningless.

Arithmetic is the first layer of trust. If the small calculations are sloppy, the advanced work has no foundation.

## Why This Idea Exists

Humans invented number systems because counting objects was not enough. Builders, traders, astronomers, navigators, engineers, and scientists needed ways to compare quantities, measure the world, predict outcomes, and communicate results.

Units exist because a bare number is incomplete. The number `12` could mean `12 volts`, `12 meters`, `12 seconds`, `12 grams`, or `12 dollars`. In technical work, those are completely different things.

Standard units exist because people need measurements to be portable. A circuit calculation, a machine drawing, a physics paper, or a drone mission plan should not depend on one person's private measuring system.

Estimation exists because exact calculation is not enough. A calculator can produce a precise-looking wrong answer. Estimation gives you a way to ask: "Does this answer live in the right neighborhood?"

## Plain-Language Intuition

Think of every technical number as a label on something real.

`5` by itself is just a number.

`5 meters` is a length.

`5 seconds` is a time.

`5 amps` is electrical current.

`5 volts` is electrical potential difference.

The unit changes what the number means.

Arithmetic combines quantities. When you combine them, the units should tell a story that makes sense.

If you multiply speed by time, you should get distance:

$$\frac{meters}{second} \times seconds = meters$$

If your result has the wrong kind of unit, your formula is probably wrong.

## Core Theory

### Quantities

A measured quantity has a number and a unit:

$$quantity = number \times unit$$

Examples:

- `12 V`
- `3.5 A`
- `40 m`
- `2 h`
- `250 g`

The number tells "how much." The unit tells "of what."

### Basic Operations

Addition and subtraction require compatible units:

$$3m + 2m = 5m$$

But this is not meaningful:

$$3m + 2s$$

Meters and seconds measure different kinds of things.

Multiplication and division create new unit combinations:

$$speed = \frac{distance}{time}$$

$$power = voltage \times current$$

$$area = length \times width$$

### Place Value

Place value is how our decimal number system stores size.

In `4,582.63`:

- `4` means four thousands.
- `5` means five hundreds.
- `8` means eight tens.
- `2` means two ones.
- `6` means six tenths.
- `3` means three hundredths.

Place value is why moving a decimal point changes magnitude by powers of ten.

### Powers of Ten

Technical work often moves between very small and very large quantities.

$$10^3 = 1000$$

$$10^{-3} = 0.001$$

That matters because metric prefixes are powers of ten.

Common prefixes:

| Prefix | Symbol | Multiplier | Example |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- |
| kilo | k | 1,000 | `1 km = 1000 m` |
| centi | c | 0.01 | `1 cm = 0.01 m` |
| milli | m | 0.001 | `1 mA = 0.001 A` |
| micro | u | 0.000001 | `1 us = 0.000001 s` |

Note: the official micro symbol is the Greek letter mu. This repo uses `u` in plain text when ASCII is safer.

### Scientific Notation

Scientific notation writes a number as:

$$a \times 10^n$$

where `a` is at least `1` and less than `10`.

Examples:

$$4500 = 4.5 \times 10^3$$

$$0.0032 = 3.2 \times 10^{-3}$$

Scientific notation makes very large and very small values easier to compare.

### Estimation

Estimation is not guessing randomly. It is controlled simplification.

You might round numbers to nearby values:

$$19.8 \approx 20$$

$$4.9 \approx 5$$

Then:

$$19.8 \times 4.9 \approx 20 \times 5 = 100$$

If the exact calculation returns `970.2`, you know something is wrong.

### Accuracy, Precision, And Uncertainty

Accuracy means closeness to the true value.

Precision means how repeatable or finely resolved a measurement is.

Uncertainty describes the range where the true value may reasonably be.

For example:

$$L = 12.4cm \pm 0.1cm$$

This means the measured length is about `12.4 cm`, with uncertainty of `0.1 cm`.

This matters because measured numbers are not magic. A sensor, ruler, multimeter, GPS receiver, or camera calibration has limits.

## Mental Model

Use a four-question check every time you calculate:

1. What kind of thing am I calculating?
2. What units should the answer have?
3. What size should the answer roughly be?
4. Does the final answer make physical sense?

If you cannot answer those questions, slow down. The calculation is not ready.

## Worked Example 1: Robot Distance

A rover moves at `0.6 m/s` for `20 s`. How far does it travel?

Estimate:

`0.6` is a little more than half a meter per second. For `20 seconds`, the distance should be a little more than `10 m`.

Formula:

$$distance = speed \times time$$

Substitute:

$$distance = 0.6\frac{m}{s} \times 20s$$

Cancel seconds:

$$distance = 12m$$

Reasonableness check: `12 m` is a little more than `10 m`, so the answer makes sense.

## Worked Example 2: Sensor Battery Use

A sensor draws `50 mA` for `4 hours`. How many amp-hours does it use?

Convert milliamps to amps:

$$50mA = 50 \times 0.001A = 0.05A$$

Charge use:

$$charge = current \times time$$

$$charge = 0.05A \times 4h = 0.20Ah$$

The sensor uses `0.20 Ah`.

Reasonableness check: `50 mA` is a small current. Over `4 hours`, the result should still be much less than `1 Ah`. `0.20 Ah` makes sense.

## Worked Example 3: Catching A Bad Decimal

A motor draws `1.2 A` for `30 minutes`. Someone calculates:

$$1.2 \times 30 = 36Ah$$

This is wrong because `30 minutes` was not converted to hours.

Correct conversion:

$$30min = 0.5h$$

Correct calculation:

$$1.2A \times 0.5h = 0.6Ah$$

The bad answer was larger by a factor of `60`.

## Worked Example 4: Unit Mismatch

Can you add `3 meters + 4 seconds`?

No. The units measure different dimensions. You can write the expression, but it does not represent a meaningful physical quantity.

You can add:

$$3m + 4m = 7m$$

You can divide:

$$\frac{3m}{4s} = 0.75m/s$$

Division creates speed. Addition does not.

## Engineering Connections

### Circuits

Current, voltage, resistance, power, and energy all depend on arithmetic with units.

$$P = IV$$

If current is in amps and voltage is in volts, power is in watts.

### Drones

Flight time, ground speed, camera footprint, battery capacity, and GPS distance require unit conversions and sanity checks.

### Robotics

Robot motion uses distance, time, speed, acceleration, encoder counts, gear ratios, and coordinate changes.

### Computer Vision

Pixel measurements become real-world measurements only when scale and units are handled correctly.

### Physics

Physics is full of quantities with units. Unit mistakes are often the first sign that a derivation or simulation is wrong.

## Common Failure Modes

### Failure Mode 1: Bare Numbers

Writing `12` instead of `12 V` hides meaning.

Fix: attach units to every measured quantity.

### Failure Mode 2: Minute-Hour Confusion

Battery capacity is often in amp-hours, but time may be given in minutes.

Fix:

$$minutes \div 60 = hours$$

### Failure Mode 3: Milli-Confusion

`mA` and `A` differ by `1000`.

Fix:

$$1mA = 0.001A$$

### Failure Mode 4: Trusting Calculator Output

A calculator does not know your physical system.

Fix: estimate before and after.

### Challenge
