# Signed Numbers and Order of Operations

## Re-entry Promise

This lesson assumes negative numbers and calculation order may feel slippery. That is normal. Signed numbers are not just a school topic; they are how technical systems represent direction, error, offset, debt, temperature, voltage polarity, altitude change, and coordinate position.

We will rebuild the meaning first, then the rules.

## Objective

By the end, you should be able to:

- Explain positive and negative numbers using a number line.
- Distinguish subtraction from a negative value.
- Understand opposite and absolute value.
- Apply order of operations without relying on vague memory.
- Evaluate expressions with negative numbers, parentheses, exponents, multiplication, division, addition, and subtraction.
- Connect sign conventions to robots, drones, circuits, coordinate systems, and physics.

## Why It Matters

Physical systems need direction. Without signs, you cannot clearly represent:

- left vs right,
- forward vs backward,
- up vs down,
- charging vs discharging,
- positive vs negative voltage,
- above vs below sea level,
- error above or below a target,
- clockwise vs counterclockwise rotation.

Order of operations matters because computers, calculators, spreadsheets, firmware, and simulations all evaluate expressions according to rules. If you write the expression wrong, the machine will confidently compute the wrong thing.

## Why This Idea Exists

Positive numbers are natural when counting objects. Negative numbers took longer to become accepted because they are less obvious physically. But once people needed to represent debts, losses, below-zero temperatures, direction, and algebraic balance, negative numbers became unavoidable.

Order of operations exists because mathematical expressions need shared grammar. Without conventions, the expression:

$$2 + 3 \times 4$$

could mean:

$$(2 + 3) \times 4 = 20$$

or:

$$2 + (3 \times 4) = 14$$

The convention removes ambiguity.

## Plain-Language Intuition

A sign tells you which side of zero you are on or which direction you are moving.

On a number line:

- positive numbers are to the right of zero,
- negative numbers are to the left of zero,
- zero is the reference point.

If east is positive, then west is negative.

If up is positive, then down is negative.

If a target error is:

$$error = target - measured$$

then a positive error and a negative error mean different correction directions.

## Core Theory

### Integers

Integers are whole numbers and their opposites:

$$..., -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, ...$$

They are useful for counting steps, positions, direction changes, and discrete quantities.

### Opposite

The opposite of a number is the same distance from zero on the other side.

The opposite of `5` is `-5`.

The opposite of `-5` is `5`.

Mathematically:

$$-(-5) = 5$$

### Absolute Value

Absolute value is distance from zero:

$$|n|$$

Distance is never negative.

$$|-7| = 7$$

$$|7| = 7$$

In engineering, absolute value can represent magnitude without direction.

### Adding Signed Numbers

Adding a positive number moves right on the number line.

Adding a negative number moves left.

$$3 + (-5) = -2$$

This is the same as:

$$3 - 5 = -2$$

### Subtracting Signed Numbers

Subtracting a number means adding its opposite.

$$a - b = a + (-b)$$

Example:

$$4 - (-3) = 4 + 3 = 7$$

Subtracting a negative increases the value because you are removing a movement to the left.

### Multiplying Signed Numbers

Rules:

- positive times positive is positive,
- positive times negative is negative,
- negative times positive is negative,
- negative times negative is positive.

Why is negative times negative positive? One intuition: multiplying by a negative reverses direction. Reversing a reversal points you forward again.

$$(-3)(-4) = 12$$

### Dividing Signed Numbers

Division follows the same sign pattern as multiplication:

$$\frac{-12}{3} = -4$$

$$\frac{-12}{-3} = 4$$

### Order Of Operations

Use this order:

1. Parentheses and grouping
2. Exponents
3. Multiplication and division from left to right
4. Addition and subtraction from left to right

The left-to-right part matters. Multiplication does not always happen before division. They share a level. Addition and subtraction also share a level.

### Parentheses And Negative Bases

This distinction matters:

$$-3^2 = -9$$

because the exponent applies to `3`, then the negative sign is applied.

But:

$$(-3)^2 = 9$$

because the base is the entire signed number `-3`.

This is a common source of mistakes in calculators and code.

## Mental Models

### Number Line As Position

Imagine a robot on a line:

- `0` is the starting dock.
- positive means east.
- negative means west.

If the robot starts at `-3`, moves `+8`, then moves `-2`:

$$-3 + 8 - 2 = 3$$

It ends at position `+3`.

### Sign As Direction

A sign is not always "good" or "bad." It often means direction relative to a convention.

If altitude change is positive upward:

- `+20 m` means climb,
- `-20 m` means descend.

### Parentheses As Packaging

Parentheses package a value or expression so it is treated as one thing.

`(-5)` is a packaged negative five.

`-(5^2)` means the negative of twenty-five.

## Worked Example 1: Robot Position

A robot starts at `x = -4 m`. It moves `+10 m`, then `-3 m`.

$$x = -4 + 10 - 3$$

First:

$$-4 + 10 = 6$$

Then:

$$6 - 3 = 3$$

The robot ends at `x = 3 m`.

## Worked Example 2: Temperature Change

A battery test chamber is at `-8 C`. It warms by `15 C`.

$$-8 + 15 = 7$$

The chamber reaches `7 C`.

The sign tells position relative to zero Celsius.

## Worked Example 3: Correcting Sensor Offset

A pressure sensor has an offset of `-0.20 V`. A raw reading is `2.10 V`. If corrected value is:

$$corrected = raw - offset$$

then:

$$corrected = 2.10 - (-0.20)$$

$$corrected = 2.30V$$

Subtracting a negative offset increased the corrected value.

## Worked Example 4: Order Of Operations

Evaluate:

$$12 - 3 \times 2$$

Multiplication first:

$$3 \times 2 = 6$$

Then subtraction:

$$12 - 6 = 6$$

Now compare:

$$(12 - 3) \times 2$$

Parentheses first:

$$12 - 3 = 9$$

Then multiplication:

$$9 \times 2 = 18$$

Parentheses changed the meaning.

## Worked Example 5: Negative Exponent Pitfall

Evaluate:

$$-4^2$$

Exponent first:

$$4^2 = 16$$

Then apply the negative:

$$-4^2 = -16$$

But:

$$(-4)^2 = 16$$

The parentheses change the base.

## Worked Example 6: Control Error

A robot arm target angle is `30 degrees`. The measured angle is `34 degrees`.

$$error = target - measured$$

$$error = 30 - 34 = -4 degrees$$

The negative error says the arm is 4 degrees beyond the target in the positive direction, so the correction should move opposite that direction.

## Common Failure Modes

### Failure Mode 1: Losing The Negative Sign

`-3^2` and `(-3)^2` are not the same.

Fix: use parentheses when the negative value is intended to be the base.

### Failure Mode 2: Treating Subtracting A Negative As Subtracting

$$5 - (-2)$$

is:

$$5 + 2 = 7$$

Fix: rewrite subtraction as adding the opposite.

### Failure Mode 3: Forgetting Left-To-Right

Multiplication and division are evaluated left to right. Addition and subtraction are evaluated left to right.

Fix: do not treat PEMDAS as if multiplication always beats division.

### Failure Mode 4: Sign Convention Not Defined

If you do not define which direction is positive, a negative value may be ambiguous.

Fix: state the reference direction before calculating.

## Engineering Connections

### Robotics

Robot coordinate systems use signed positions and velocities. Error terms in control loops can be positive or negative.

### Drones

Altitude change, yaw direction, pitch, roll, and navigation offsets all depend on sign conventions.

### Circuits

Voltage polarity, current direction, signal offsets, and AC waveforms require signed quantities.

### Computer Vision

Pixel coordinate differences can be positive or negative depending on direction in the image.

### Physics

Velocity, acceleration, force, displacement, charge, potential energy changes, and fields can involve signed quantities.
