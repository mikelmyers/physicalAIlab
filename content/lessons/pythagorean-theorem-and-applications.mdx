# The Pythagorean Theorem and Applications

## Re-entry Promise

This lesson assumes you know what points, lines, segments, angles, triangles, congruence, and similarity mean. It assumes you can square numbers, take square roots, solve simple equations, and work with units. It does not assume you remember the Pythagorean theorem beyond maybe seeing $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ somewhere in school.

We will rebuild the theorem from physical intuition: right angles create independent directions. If one motion goes east and another goes north, the diagonal distance is not the sum of the distances. It is the square-root combination. That fact becomes one of the main tools for measuring distance in geometry, navigation, robotics, CAD, computer vision, game physics, structural layout, and coordinate systems.

By the end, you should not merely recite the formula. You should know when it applies, why it applies, how to use the converse, how to recognize common triples, how the distance formula comes from it, and how to catch wrong uses.

## Objective

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- Identify the legs and hypotenuse of a right triangle.
- State the Pythagorean theorem: $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ for right triangles.
- Explain why the hypotenuse must be the longest side.
- Solve for a missing leg or hypotenuse.
- Use the converse: if $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$, then the triangle is right.
- Use Pythagorean triples such as $3$-$4$-$5$, $5$-$12$-$13$, $8$-$15$-$17$, and scaled versions.
- Derive and use the coordinate distance formula.
- Apply the theorem to diagonals, ramps, robot displacement, map distances, sensor range, bracing, and clearance checks.
- Recognize when the theorem does not apply.
- Explain how the theorem bridges geometry into trigonometry and vectors.

## Why It Matters

The Pythagorean theorem is the first major distance engine in the curriculum. It turns perpendicular components into a diagonal magnitude.

1. **Robotics displacement.** If a robot moves $3$ m east and $4$ m north, its net displacement from the start is $5$ m. The path length may be $7$ m, but the straight-line displacement is $5$ m.

2. **CAD and mechanical layout.** A rectangular plate with width $w$ and height $h$ has diagonal $\sqrt{w^2+h^2}$. That diagonal matters for packaging, clearances, bracing, and material cuts.

3. **Drones and maps.** Horizontal offset and altitude form a right triangle. So do north/east coordinate differences on a local map plane.

4. **Structural bracing.** Diagonal braces in rectangular frames are sized with the Pythagorean theorem when the corner is square.

5. **Computer vision and graphics.** Pixel distances, vector lengths, camera rays, and 3D geometry all use square-root distance formulas descended from this theorem.

6. **Electronics and signals.** Orthogonal components show up in AC circuits, phasors, and signal processing. Magnitudes are often computed with the same square-root-of-squares structure.

7. **Physics.** Resultant forces, velocities, and displacements with perpendicular components use the same idea. In vector language, this becomes magnitude.

The theorem is not just about triangles. It is about measuring combined independent directions.

## Why This Idea Exists

The relationship between the sides of a right triangle was known long before Pythagoras. Babylonian tablets such as Plimpton 322 show sophisticated knowledge of integer right-triangle triples. Indian, Chinese, Greek, and other mathematical traditions all developed versions of the result.

Euclid's *Elements*, Book I Proposition 47, gives the famous geometric proof: in a right triangle, the square on the hypotenuse has area equal to the sum of the squares on the legs. Proposition 48 gives the converse: if the square on one side equals the sum of the squares on the other two, the angle opposite the longest side is right.

The theorem became central because it gives a universal measurement rule. Whenever directions are perpendicular, their squared lengths add. Later mathematics generalized this idea far beyond triangles: the distance formula in coordinates, vector magnitudes, dot products, Euclidean norm, least-squares error, and even Hilbert spaces all carry the Pythagorean pattern.

This is why the theorem appears simple and keeps returning. It is a seed crystal for a lot of serious math.

## Plain-Language Intuition

Imagine walking in a city laid out like a grid.

You walk 3 blocks east and 4 blocks north. You did not walk 5 blocks; you walked 7 blocks along the street path. But if a drone flew straight from your start to your end, it would fly 5 blocks.

The east movement and north movement are perpendicular. They do not overlap. The diagonal combines them, but not by ordinary addition. The diagonal is longer than either component but shorter than their sum.

That is the emotional shape of the theorem:

$$
\text{diagonal}^2 = \text{east}^2 + \text{north}^2
$$

The squares are not decorative. They are area-like measures of how much each perpendicular direction contributes.

## Core Theory

### Right Triangles

A **right triangle** is a triangle with one angle measuring $90^\circ$.

The two sides forming the right angle are called the **legs**. The side opposite the right angle is the **hypotenuse**.

The hypotenuse is always the longest side because it is opposite the largest angle.

Notation usually uses:

$$
a^2 + b^2 = c^2
$$

where $a$ and $b$ are legs, and $c$ is the hypotenuse.

The label $c$ must be the hypotenuse. If you put a leg in the $c$ position, the calculation will be wrong.

### The Theorem

For any right triangle with legs $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse $c$:

$$
a^2 + b^2 = c^2
$$

To find the hypotenuse:

$$
c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}
$$

To find a missing leg:

$$
a = \sqrt{c^2 - b^2}
$$

or:

$$
b = \sqrt{c^2 - a^2}
$$

Notice the subtraction when finding a leg. This is a common place for mistakes.

### Area Proof Intuition

One classic proof uses four copies of the same right triangle arranged inside a square.

Each small triangle has legs $a$ and $b$, hypotenuse $c$, and area:

$$
\frac{1}{2}ab
$$

Arrange four of them so their hypotenuses form a tilted inner square. The large outer square has side length $a+b$, so its area is:

$$
(a+b)^2
$$

The same area is also four triangle areas plus the inner square:

$$
4\left(\frac{1}{2}ab\right) + c^2
$$

Set them equal:

$$
(a+b)^2 = 2ab + c^2
$$

Expand:

$$
a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = 2ab + c^2
$$

Subtract $2ab$:

$$
a^2 + b^2 = c^2
$$

This proof matters because it shows the theorem is about area relationships, not arbitrary symbol manipulation.

### The Converse

The converse says:

If a triangle has side lengths $a$, $b$, and $c$, where $c$ is the longest side, and:

$$
a^2 + b^2 = c^2
$$

then the triangle is a right triangle.

This is useful for checking whether a corner is square. Ancient builders could use a rope marked in lengths $3$, $4$, and $5$. If those lengths formed a triangle, the angle between the $3$ and $4$ sides was a right angle.

### Acute And Obtuse Tests

For a triangle with side lengths $a \le b \le c$:

- If $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$, the triangle is right.
- If $a^2 + b^2 > c^2$, the triangle is acute.
- If $a^2 + b^2 < c^2$, the triangle is obtuse.

This lets you classify a triangle from side lengths alone.

### Pythagorean Triples

A **Pythagorean triple** is a set of positive integers that satisfy:

$$
a^2 + b^2 = c^2
$$

Common triples:

| Legs | Hypotenuse |
| --- | --- |
| $3$, $4$ | $5$ |
| $5$, $12$ | $13$ |
| $8$, $15$ | $17$ |
| $7$, $24$ | $25$ |
| $9$, $40$ | $41$ |

Scaled triples also work:

- $6$-$8$-$10$ is $2$ times $3$-$4$-$5$.
- $15$-$20$-$25$ is $5$ times $3$-$4$-$5$.
- $10$-$24$-$26$ is $2$ times $5$-$12$-$13$.

Recognizing triples saves time and reduces arithmetic errors.

### Coordinate Distance Formula

Suppose two points in the plane are:

$$
P_1 = (x_1, y_1)
$$

and:

$$
P_2 = (x_2, y_2)
$$

The horizontal change is:

$$
\Delta x = x_2 - x_1
$$

The vertical change is:

$$
\Delta y = y_2 - y_1
$$

These form the legs of a right triangle. The straight-line distance is:

$$
d = \sqrt{(\Delta x)^2 + (\Delta y)^2}
$$

So:

$$
d = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}
$$

In 3D, the same idea extends:

$$
d = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2 + (z_2-z_1)^2}
$$

That is the Pythagorean theorem applied twice.

## Visual Or Mental Models

### Model 1: Components To Magnitude

Think of perpendicular directions as independent channels. East does not contain north. North does not contain east. The total straight-line displacement combines them by square root of squared components.

This is the mental model behind vector magnitude:

$$
\lVert v \rVert = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}
$$

### Model 2: Diagonal Brace

A rectangle with width $w$ and height $h$ has diagonal:

$$
\sqrt{w^2+h^2}
$$

If you cut a brace too short, it will not reach. If you cut it too long, it will not fit. The theorem turns a right-angle layout into a cut length.

### Model 3: Distance Is Not Path Length

Walking 3 m east and 4 m north is a 7 m path but a 5 m displacement. The theorem gives straight-line separation, not total distance traveled along a broken path.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Find The Hypotenuse

A right triangle has legs $6$ cm and $8$ cm. Find the hypotenuse.

$$
c = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2}
$$

$$
c = \sqrt{36 + 64}
$$

$$
c = \sqrt{100} = 10
$$

The hypotenuse is $10$ cm.

### Example 2: Find A Missing Leg

A right triangle has hypotenuse $13$ m and one leg $5$ m. Find the other leg.

$$
a^2 + 5^2 = 13^2
$$

$$
a^2 + 25 = 169
$$

$$
a^2 = 144
$$

$$
a = 12
$$

The missing leg is $12$ m.

### Example 3: Check A Right Angle

A triangular frame has sides $9$ cm, $12$ cm, and $15$ cm. Is it right?

Longest side is $15$.

Check:

$$
9^2 + 12^2 = 81 + 144 = 225
$$

$$
15^2 = 225
$$

Since they match, the triangle is right.

### Example 4: Rectangle Diagonal

A rectangular electronics panel is $40$ cm wide and $30$ cm tall. What is its diagonal?

$$
d = \sqrt{40^2 + 30^2}
$$

$$
d = \sqrt{1600 + 900}
$$

$$
d = \sqrt{2500} = 50
$$

The diagonal is $50$ cm. This is a scaled $3$-$4$-$5$ triangle.

### Example 5: Robot Displacement

A robot moves $2.4$ m east and $3.2$ m north. How far is it from its start?

$$
d = \sqrt{2.4^2 + 3.2^2}
$$

$$
d = \sqrt{5.76 + 10.24}
$$

$$
d = \sqrt{16} = 4
$$

The robot is $4$ m from its start.

Its path length was $2.4 + 3.2 = 5.6$ m if it drove in two straight segments. Displacement is different.

### Example 6: Coordinate Distance

Find the distance between:

$$
A = (2, -1)
$$

and:

$$
B = (8, 7)
$$

Compute changes:

$$
\Delta x = 8 - 2 = 6
$$

$$
\Delta y = 7 - (-1) = 8
$$

Distance:

$$
d = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{100} = 10
$$

The points are $10$ units apart.

### Example 7: Drone Slant Range

A drone is horizontally $120$ m from a ground marker and $50$ m above the ground. What is the slant range from marker to drone?

$$
r = \sqrt{120^2 + 50^2}
$$

$$
r = \sqrt{14400 + 2500}
$$

$$
r = \sqrt{16900} = 130
$$

The slant range is $130$ m. This is a scaled $5$-$12$-$13$ triangle.

### Example 8: Classify A Triangle

Classify the triangle with side lengths $6$, $7$, and $10$.

Longest side is $10$.

$$
6^2 + 7^2 = 36 + 49 = 85
$$

$$
10^2 = 100
$$

Since $85 < 100$, the triangle is obtuse.

### Example 9: Brace Length With Units

A rectangular frame is $1.2$ m wide and $0.9$ m tall. Find the diagonal brace length.

$$
d = \sqrt{1.2^2 + 0.9^2}
$$

$$
d = \sqrt{1.44 + 0.81}
$$

$$
d = \sqrt{2.25} = 1.5
$$

The brace length is $1.5$ m, ignoring hardware allowances.

### Example 10: Failure Mode - Wrong Side As Hypotenuse

A student sees side lengths $7$, $24$, and $25$, but writes:

$$
7^2 + 25^2 = 24^2
$$

This is wrong because $25$ is the longest side, so it must be the hypotenuse candidate.

Correct check:

$$
7^2 + 24^2 = 25^2
$$

$$
49 + 576 = 625
$$

$$
625 = 625
$$

The triangle is right.

## Engineering Connections

### CAD And Packaging

A diagonal dimension determines whether a board fits through an enclosure opening, whether a panel can rotate into place, or whether a brace can span two corners. Rectangular dimensions alone are often not enough.

### Robot Odometry

Robots often track position as x and y coordinates. The straight-line error from a target is:

$$
\sqrt{e_x^2 + e_y^2}
$$

That is the Pythagorean theorem in controller clothing.

### Drone And Rangefinding

Horizontal distance, altitude, and slant range form right triangles. A rangefinder pointed at the ground, a camera tilted from horizontal, or a drone offset from a marker all produce right-triangle calculations.

### Structural Layout

The $3$-$4$-$5$ method is a practical right-angle check. Mark 3 units along one side, 4 units along another, and adjust until the diagonal between marks is 5 units. The corner is square.

### Computer Vision And Pixel Distance

A pixel displacement of $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$ has magnitude:

$$
\sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2}
$$

Feature tracking, optical flow, image residuals, and calibration errors all use this shape of calculation.

## Common Failure Modes

1. **Using the theorem on non-right triangles.** The theorem applies to right triangles. Use the converse to test if needed.

2. **Putting a leg in the hypotenuse position.** The hypotenuse is the longest side and is opposite the right angle.

3. **Adding sides instead of squares.** $3 + 4 = 7$, but the diagonal is $5$.

4. **Forgetting the square root.** If $c^2 = 100$, then $c = 10$, not $100$.

5. **Subtracting incorrectly for a missing leg.** If $c$ is known, use $c^2 - b^2$, not $b^2 - c^2$.

6. **Confusing path length with displacement.** A two-leg path is longer than the straight-line displacement.

7. **Dropping units.** If legs are in meters, the resulting length is in meters. The squares temporarily carry square meters, then the square root returns meters.

8. **Using flat distance on curved Earth too casually.** For small local distances, a plane approximation is fine. For long geodesic distances, Earth curvature matters.
