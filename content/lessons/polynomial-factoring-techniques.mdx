# Polynomial Factoring Techniques

## Re-entry Promise

This lesson assumes you can multiply expressions like $(x+2)(x-3)$ and recognize polynomial terms. It does not assume factoring feels automatic.

Factoring is reverse multiplication. It rewrites a polynomial as a product of simpler pieces. That rewrite matters because products reveal zeros, cancellations, domain restrictions, and structure that expanded form hides.

By the end, you should be able to factor common polynomial patterns and explain why factoring is useful for solving equations, simplifying formulas, and understanding system behavior.

## Objective

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- Explain factoring as reverse distribution.
- Factor out a greatest common factor.
- Factor by grouping.
- Factor simple trinomials $x^2+bx+c$.
- Factor trinomials with leading coefficient not equal to 1.
- Recognize difference of squares.
- Recognize perfect-square trinomials.
- Use factoring to solve polynomial equations.
- Connect factors to roots and zero-product logic.
- Use factoring in engineering algebra, transfer functions, calibration models, and stability reasoning.

## Plain-Language Intuition

Expanded form shows how terms add. Factored form shows what can make the whole expression zero.

Compare:

$$
x^2-5x+6
$$

and:

$$
(x-2)(x-3)
$$

They are the same polynomial, but the second form immediately reveals roots 2 and 3.

Factoring is like taking a machine apart so you can see its modules.

## Why This Idea Exists

Factoring lets us solve equations and simplify structure:

- A projectile equation can be factored to find launch and landing times.
- A transfer function can be factored to reveal poles and zeros.
- A robot trajectory polynomial can be factored to identify boundary times.
- A circuit formula can be simplified by canceling common factors.
- A numerical method can exploit factored form for stability and speed.

Factoring is not just school algebra. It is structural debugging.

## Core Theory

### Zero Product Property

If:

$$
AB=0
$$

then:

$$
A=0 \quad \text{or} \quad B=0
$$

This is why factoring solves equations.

If:

$$
(x-2)(x+5)=0
$$

then:

$$
x=2 \quad \text{or} \quad x=-5
$$

### Greatest Common Factor

Always check for a greatest common factor first.

Example:

$$
6x^3+9x^2=3x^2(2x+3)
$$

The shared factor is $3x^2$.

### Factoring By Grouping

Group terms so each pair has a common factor.

Example:

$$
x^3+2x^2+3x+6
$$

Group:

$$
(x^3+2x^2)+(3x+6)
$$

Factor each group:

$$
x^2(x+2)+3(x+2)
$$

Factor common binomial:

$$
(x+2)(x^2+3)
$$

### Trinomials With Leading Coefficient 1

For:

$$
x^2+bx+c
$$

find two numbers that multiply to $c$ and add to $b$.

Example:

$$
x^2+5x+6
$$

Numbers 2 and 3 multiply to 6 and add to 5:

$$
(x+2)(x+3)
$$

### Trinomials With Leading Coefficient Not 1

Example:

$$
2x^2+7x+3
$$

Use trial factors or the $ac$ method. Here:

$$
2x^2+7x+3=(2x+1)(x+3)
$$

Check:

$$
2x^2+6x+x+3=2x^2+7x+3
$$

### Difference Of Squares

Pattern:

$$
a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b)
$$

Examples:

$$
x^2-9=(x-3)(x+3)
$$

$$
4x^2-25=(2x-5)(2x+5)
$$

### Perfect-Square Trinomials

Patterns:

$$
a^2+2ab+b^2=(a+b)^2
$$

$$
a^2-2ab+b^2=(a-b)^2
$$

Examples:

$$
x^2+6x+9=(x+3)^2
$$

$$
x^2-10x+25=(x-5)^2
$$

### Solving By Factoring

Solve:

$$
x^2-5x+6=0
$$

Factor:

$$
(x-2)(x-3)=0
$$

So:

$$
x=2,\quad x=3
$$

## Visual Or Mental Model

Factoring asks: "What reusable pieces could have been multiplied to produce this?"

For roots, imagine each factor as a switch. If any factor becomes zero, the whole product becomes zero. Expanded form hides the switches. Factored form exposes them.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: GCF

Factor:

$$
12x^4-8x^3
$$

Shared factor is $4x^3$:

$$
4x^3(3x-2)
$$

### Example 2: Simple Trinomial

Factor:

$$
x^2-7x+12
$$

Numbers multiply to 12 and add to -7: -3 and -4.

$$
(x-3)(x-4)
$$

### Example 3: Leading Coefficient

Factor:

$$
3x^2+10x+3
$$

$$
(3x+1)(x+3)
$$

Check:

$$
3x^2+9x+x+3=3x^2+10x+3
$$

### Example 4: Difference Of Squares

$$
9x^2-16=(3x-4)(3x+4)
$$

### Example 5: Perfect Square

$$
x^2+12x+36=(x+6)^2
$$

### Example 6: Grouping

$$
xy+2x+3y+6
$$

Group:

$$
x(y+2)+3(y+2)=(x+3)(y+2)
$$

### Example 7: Solve A Factored Equation

$$
2x(x-5)=0
$$

So:

$$
x=0,\quad x=5
$$

### Example 8: Projectile Landing Time

Height:

$$
h(t)=-5t^2+20t
$$

Factor:

$$
h(t)=-5t(t-4)
$$

Ground occurs when $h(t)=0$:

$$
t=0,\quad t=4
$$

The object launches at 0 s and lands at 4 s.

## Engineering Connections

### Projectiles

Factoring height equations reveals launch and landing times when ground height is zero.

### Transfer Functions

Factored numerator and denominator polynomials reveal zeros and poles, central to control systems and filters.

### Circuits

Factoring can simplify symbolic impedance or gain expressions before numerical evaluation.

### Calibration

Polynomial calibration equations may factor to reveal physically meaningful operating points.

### Robotics Trajectories

Boundary constraints often create polynomial factors like $t(T-t)$ that enforce zero at endpoints.

## Common Failure Modes

1. Forgetting to factor out the GCF first.
2. Getting signs wrong in trinomials.
3. Confusing difference of squares with sum of squares.
4. Factoring $x^2+9$ over the reals as if it were difference of squares.
5. Dropping a factor.
6. Not checking by multiplying back.
7. Using zero-product property before setting the equation equal to zero.
8. Dividing by a variable factor and losing a root.
9. Treating irreducible over the reals as impossible or wrong.
10. Forgetting units or physical domain after solving.
