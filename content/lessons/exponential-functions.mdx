# Exponential Functions

## Re-entry Promise

This lesson assumes you understand functions, powers like $2^3$, and basic graph reading. It does not assume exponential growth already feels intuitive.

An exponential function is what you use when change happens by repeated multiplication. Linear functions add the same amount each step. Exponential functions multiply by the same factor each step. That difference is the reason battery discharge, radioactive decay, capacitor charging, population growth, compound interest, epidemics, and algorithmic scaling can surprise people.

By the end, you should be able to recognize exponential growth and decay, interpret the base, use the natural base $e$, compute doubling/half-life behavior, and connect exponentials to physical systems.

## Objective

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- Define an exponential function.
- Distinguish exponential functions from linear and polynomial functions.
- Interpret $a$, $b$, and $x$ in $f(x)=ab^x$.
- Identify growth when $b>1$ and decay when $0<b<1$.
- Evaluate exponential functions.
- Interpret percent growth and decay factors.
- Explain why the natural base $e$ appears in continuous growth.
- Compute doubling time and half-life from simple models.
- Model capacitor charge/discharge, population growth, depreciation, and algorithmic scale.
- Recognize common exponential mistakes.

## Plain-Language Intuition

Linear change says:

> Add the same amount each step.

Exponential change says:

> Multiply by the same factor each step.

If a robot travels 2 meters more every second, that is linear. If a population doubles every hour, that is exponential. If a battery keeps 90% of its charge each hour, that is exponential decay.

Exponential functions are not "fast" by definition at the beginning. They become powerful because multiplication compounds.

## Why This Idea Exists

Exponential functions exist because many systems change in proportion to their current amount:

- More bacteria means more bacteria reproducing.
- More radioactive atoms means more atoms available to decay.
- More money in an account means more money earning interest.
- More charge difference across a capacitor means more current at that moment.
- More error in some systems can mean larger correction.

The phrase "proportional to current amount" is the hidden engine behind exponentials.

## Core Theory

### Definition

An exponential function has the variable in the exponent:

$$
f(x)=ab^x
$$

where:

- $a$ is the initial value when $x=0$,
- $b$ is the growth or decay factor per unit step,
- $b>0$ and $b\ne1$.

If $b=1$, the function is constant.

This form comes from repeated multiplication.

If a quantity starts at $a$ and gets multiplied by $b$ once per step, then:

$$
f(0)=a
$$

After one step:

$$
f(1)=ab
$$

After two steps:

$$
f(2)=abb=ab^2
$$

After three steps:

$$
f(3)=abbb=ab^3
$$

So after $x$ steps:

$$
f(x)=ab^x
$$

The exponent counts how many times the multiplier has been applied. That is the core idea of every exponential model.

### Growth And Decay

If:

$$
b>1
$$

the function grows.

If:

$$
0<b<1
$$

the function decays.

Examples:

$$
100(1.2)^t
$$

grows by 20% per unit time.

$$
100(0.8)^t
$$

decays by 20% per unit time.

### Percent Change To Factor

A 15% increase means multiply by:

$$
1+0.15=1.15
$$

A 15% decrease means multiply by:

$$
1-0.15=0.85
$$

Do not put 15 in the base. Percent must be converted to a decimal fraction.

### Evaluating

Let:

$$
f(t)=50(1.1)^t
$$

Then:

$$
f(0)=50
$$

$$
f(2)=50(1.1)^2=50(1.21)=60.5
$$

### Natural Exponential

The number $e$ is approximately:

$$
e\approx2.71828
$$

The natural exponential is:

$$
f(t)=Ae^{kt}
$$

If $k>0$, growth. If $k<0$, decay.

$e$ appears when change is continuous instead of step-by-step. In calculus, $e^x$ is the unique exponential whose derivative is itself:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}e^x=e^x
$$

That property is why $e$ becomes the default base in physics, differential equations, signal processing, and machine learning.

One way to see where $e$ comes from is continuous compounding.

Suppose a quantity grows by 100% over one time unit, but the growth is split into smaller and smaller pieces.

If growth happens once:

$$
\left(1+\frac{1}{1}\right)^1=2
$$

If growth is split into 2 half-steps:

$$
\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\right)^2=2.25
$$

If split into 10 pieces:

$$
\left(1+\frac{1}{10}\right)^{10}\approx2.594
$$

If split into 100 pieces:

$$
\left(1+\frac{1}{100}\right)^{100}\approx2.705
$$

As the pieces become infinitely small, the value approaches:

$$
e=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)^n
$$

So $e$ is not a random constant. It is the natural base for growth that is compounded continuously instead of in visible steps.

The model:

$$
A(t)=A_0e^{kt}
$$

means the amount changes at a rate proportional to its current amount. The sign of $k$ tells direction:

- $k>0$: proportional growth,
- $k<0$: proportional decay,
- larger $|k|$: faster change.

### Doubling Time

If a quantity doubles every $T$ units, a model can be written:

$$
A(t)=A_0 2^{t/T}
$$

Example: starting at 10 and doubling every 3 hours:

$$
A(t)=10\cdot2^{t/3}
$$

Why does the exponent use $t/T$?

The fraction $t/T$ counts how many doubling periods have passed. If $T=3$ hours:

- at $t=0$, $t/T=0$, so there have been no doublings,
- at $t=3$, $t/T=1$, so there has been one doubling,
- at $t=6$, $t/T=2$, so there have been two doublings,
- at $t=1.5$, $t/T=0.5$, so the model is halfway through one continuous doubling period.

That is why:

$$
A(t)=A_0 2^{t/T}
$$

The base 2 says "double." The exponent says "how many doublings."

### Half-Life

If a quantity halves every $T$ units:

$$
A(t)=A_0\left(\frac12\right)^{t/T}
$$

Example: starting at 80 and half-life 5 days:

$$
A(t)=80\left(\frac12\right)^{t/5}
$$

The same reasoning applies. The base $\frac12$ says "halve." The exponent $t/T$ says "how many half-life periods have passed."

After one half-life:

$$
A(T)=A_0\left(\frac12\right)^1=\frac{A_0}{2}
$$

After two half-lives:

$$
A(2T)=A_0\left(\frac12\right)^2=\frac{A_0}{4}
$$

This is why radioactive decay, drug clearance, and capacitor discharge are often described by half-life-like quantities: humans understand "time to halve" more easily than an abstract decay constant.

### Exponential Versus Polynomial

Polynomial:

$$
x^3
$$

Exponential:

$$
3^x
$$

The variable's position matters. In a polynomial, the variable is the base. In an exponential, the variable is in the exponent.

## Visual Or Mental Model

Think of an exponential as a repeated multiplier machine.

Start with $a$. Each step, multiply by $b$.

If $b=2$, the values go:

$$
a,\ 2a,\ 4a,\ 8a,\ 16a
$$

If $b=0.5$, the values go:

$$
a,\ 0.5a,\ 0.25a,\ 0.125a
$$

The graph of exponential decay approaches zero but never reaches it in the ideal model.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Growth Factor

A quantity grows by 8% per year. The growth factor is:

$$
1.08
$$

If initial value is 200:

$$
A(t)=200(1.08)^t
$$

### Example 2: Decay Factor

A sensor's calibration confidence decays by 12% per month. The factor is:

$$
0.88
$$

Model:

$$
C(t)=C_0(0.88)^t
$$

### Example 3: Evaluate Growth

Let:

$$
P(t)=100(1.05)^t
$$

After 3 time steps:

$$
P(3)=100(1.05)^3\approx115.76
$$

### Example 4: Half-Life

A material starts at 64 units and halves every 2 hours:

$$
A(t)=64\left(\frac12\right)^{t/2}
$$

After 6 hours:

$$
A(6)=64\left(\frac12\right)^3=8
$$

### Example 5: Doubling Time

A bacteria culture starts with 500 cells and doubles every 4 hours:

$$
N(t)=500\cdot2^{t/4}
$$

After 12 hours:

$$
N(12)=500\cdot2^3=4000
$$

### Example 6: Capacitor Discharge

A simple capacitor discharge has form:

$$
V(t)=V_0e^{-t/RC}
$$

If $V_0=5$ V and $RC=2$ s:

$$
V(t)=5e^{-t/2}
$$

At $t=2$ s:

$$
V(2)=5e^{-1}\approx1.84\text{ V}
$$

### Example 7: Algorithmic Scale

An exhaustive binary search tree with depth $n$ can have about:

$$
2^n
$$

leaves. Increasing depth from 20 to 30 multiplies leaves by:

$$
2^{10}=1024
$$

Ten extra levels can be a thousandfold cost increase.

### Example 8: Failure Mode

A 20% decay is not:

$$
A(t)=A_0(20)^t
$$

It is:

$$
A(t)=A_0(0.8)^t
$$

Percent must be converted into a multiplier.

## Engineering Connections

### Electronics

Capacitor and inductor transients use exponentials. The time constant controls how quickly the system approaches its final value.

### Robotics Batteries

Battery state, thermal derating, and degradation can be modeled with exponential-like curves over limited ranges.

### Machine Learning

Exponential moving averages, softmax, and loss scaling all use exponentials.

### Reliability

Failure and survival models often use exponential decay as a first approximation.

### Algorithms

Exponential search spaces are why brute force becomes impossible.

## Common Failure Modes

1. Confusing $x^3$ with $3^x$.
2. Treating percent as a whole number instead of a decimal.
3. Using $1+r$ for decay instead of $1-r$.
4. Thinking exponential decay reaches zero exactly.
5. Forgetting the initial value $a$.
6. Assuming all fast growth is exponential.
7. Ignoring units on time.
8. Mixing step-based and continuous models.
9. Treating $e$ as magic instead of the continuous-growth base.
10. Extrapolating an exponential model far beyond its valid range.
